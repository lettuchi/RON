# Wellness District Compliance (Modern AU Case 1)

**Bonus prose · not canon.** Third person, mostly Toa POV. Rom-com bureau noir; optional R18 stays tasteful and framed as a sponsorship audit, not dating on letterhead. Play the VN episode: `game/au_modern_case1_compliance.rpy`. Prequel: [Wrong Floor (Modern AU)](./ryoko-owari-au-modern-wrong-floor.md).

---

Three business days after the wrong conference room, Kakita Toa learned that a deputy director's chop did not only open doors at the Ryoko Port Arts Council. It opened field trips.

The chop in question belonged to Kitsu Kaoru, Deputy Director at the Office of Arts & Licensing, the permanent office that did the Council's actual paperwork. It processed applications, renewed the permits that let artists like her rehearse and get paid in the port, licensed the venues and storefronts those artists worked in, ran the inspections nobody remembered authorizing, and, when it felt like ruining a Thursday, sent a deputy out into the rain to do one in person. His signature was the only reason she was allowed to dance for money in this city at all. She was learning that a signature like that came attached to errands.

He met her at the licensing tower before dawn, which was his way of saying *the queue starts when I say it starts*. Rain still owned the harbor. Toa wore the black rehearsal tracksuit, gold stripes down the legs, the gold crane stamped over her heart like a logo she had not chosen but had stopped resenting, because every other outfit in her bag still apologized in the shoulders.

"Mandatory on-site witness," she read off her tablet, for the fourth time, as if the clause might soften if she caught it tired. "That sounds like liability with fluorescent lighting."

"It sounds like exactly what it is," Kaoru said. "You attend. You document. You do not perform gratitude on municipal letterhead." A beat. "Again."

"I performed gratitude once."

"You stamped a paper plate with your personal chop. The Council has a long memory and a worse filing system. It kept the worst half."

She let that go, because arguing with him before sunrise was its own form of cardio. The witness clause was the price of a fast sponsorship, and she had read the price often enough to recite it. The Council, the Ryoko Port Arts Council, the board of councilors who funded and sanctioned every performing-arts sponsorship in the port, did not hand out fast tracks for free. When a deputy signed an artist through ahead of the queue, somebody upstairs eventually asked why. The clause was his answer, drafted in advance of the question. She would attend the inspections he ran. She would document what he pointed at. And she would stand on the public record as living proof that the deputy who had vouched for her had not invented a dancer to pad his own numbers. Favoritism insurance, written for a favoritism nobody had committed yet. The fine print was blunt about what it cost to skip: *failure to attend revokes sponsor standing*. Miss an inspection and the signature that let her stay in Ryoko Port stopped meaning anything, and a lapsed sponsorship was a one-way ferry back to the inland circuit.

The wellness corridor was a string of licensed calm: massage studios, float tanks, candle rhetoric under one shared neon awning. Velvet Lantern Wellness Collective promised serenity in four competing fonts. What the awning did not advertise was the paperwork holding it up. Every storefront on the strip operated under a premises license, the same operating license the Office of Arts & Licensing issued to the studios and small theaters it sponsored, because the wellness corridor adjoined the licensed-arts district and lived off its foot traffic. That put the whole strip squarely inside Kaoru's remit. The office that signed artists also licensed the storefronts those artists walked past, which meant the office that signed artists could walk into those storefronts unannounced, cite them, and feed the citations up to the board that could suspend or pull their license to keep the doors open at all. Velvet Lantern promised serenity. Kaoru promised occupancy charts, fire exits, and a sponsor artist on the witness log.

Toa was the sponsor artist.

She had imagined witness work as a courtroom bench from television: mahogany rails, objections, someone in a robe shouting *sustained*. Ryoko Port filed witnesses the way it filed rain, quietly and in quantity, onto a record nobody read until they needed it to end somebody. Her clause asked for three things and called none of them dramatic: presence, documentation, and the kind of stillness that did not read as performance when a municipal camera was running.

Kaoru had not sold her the spa chain in metaphors. He had sent a calendar invite through the Council's portal, the same portal that held her sponsorship file and his signature on it, with a map pin, a start time, and a single line of body text that ended *failure to attend revokes sponsor standing*. No greeting. No emoji, the way a ward office might. She had accepted it at midnight, slept badly, dreamed of elevator doors closing on a floor that was not on the public map, and still arrived early, because to Toa lateness was just apology wearing a watch.

Pink neon turned the wet sidewalk into a brochure nobody had requested. Kaoru walked toward it like the inspection was already filed and only the citations remained to be typed. His clipboard was municipal grey, the cheap kind the office bought in bulk. Her phone was personal black. Both would end up in the same compliance PDF by lunch, his observations and her footage stapled into one report under a case number she would never be handed.

Inside, the front-desk host smiled the way you smile at a seasonal allergy, politely, and hoping it would pass. Kaoru did not smile back. He set his badge on the counter, the one that named the office out loud, and let the host read it.

"Surprise inspection," he told the lobby, not the host, because the lobby was the thing on the license. "No appointment, and you don't get to schedule one. A premises license means we can walk in unannounced. If you scrub the lobby while I'm standing in it, we note the scrub, and the scrub becomes its own line in the report."

Toa raised her phone, screen already open to the camera. "Do I film everything, or only what you point at? My storage is personal, not municipal, and my storage is full."

"You film what I tell you is exhibit-worthy, and nothing your storage will regret. Flirt with the lens on your own time, To-chan. On mine, it is evidence or it is clutter, and clutter does not survive an audit."

The lobby smelled like eucalyptus and money pretending to be mindfulness. Soft music laid itself over the harbor like a second awning, hiding the diesel and the ferries under panpipes. Toa noticed how fast the staff moved the second Kaoru uncapped his pen: chairs squared to invisible lines, diffusers dialed down, a standee about *detox journeys* rotated quietly to face the wall, as if shame could be achieved at an angle. Nobody murdered anyone. Nobody even raised a voice. The entire confrontation was conducted in paperwork and posture, and she was learning to read it the way he did, as geometry. Lines that were compliant. Lines that were not. The narrow space between them, where the citations lived.

They passed a float-tank room with a handwritten sign taped to the door: *Silence is compliance*. Toa almost laughed out loud, because the sign had no idea how close to true it was in his line of work, then she filmed it instead, because Kaoru tilted his chin at it once, economical as a stamp coming down.

The operating entity on the premises license made her bite the inside of her cheek to keep her face professional: Scorpion-grey Holdings LLC. The name sat there on his tablet, on the very line where the licensee was supposed to reassure the city it was a real and boring company.

"Cute," Kaoru said, reading it the way other people read a footnote that was about to need a lawyer. "File that under names that audition for subpoenas."

"Is that a problem or a punchline?" Toa asked.

"Both, eventually. For now it is a line item." He scrolled the license history. "Grey holding companies love grey corridors. They like premises they can lease through three shells and abandon a week before the board votes on a revocation. None of which is illegal yet, and all of which is exactly why the office reads past the first page before it renews anything. We walk the corridor anyway. We just walk it writing things down."

Witness protocol forced a small choice, the kind the clause left up to her. She could timestamp video until every chair in the building had a timecode, or she could keep her thumbs quiet and run a typed log instead. Kaoru approved either path with the same dry appetite the office gave to anything that produced a record: clocks saved hearings, and typos earned his notice, and those were the only two opinions he seemed to hold about her method.

She chose film, mostly because her hands needed something honest to do and her hands did not trust themselves to spell at this hour. The first clip ran only thirty seconds: lobby chairs, the host's smile, Kaoru's grey clipboard entering the frame like weather moving in off the harbor. The second clip was a capacity chart peeling at one corner, the posted number and the actual seating disagreeing by a margin the office would care about a great deal. By the third room her shoulders had stopped apologizing to people who had not asked them to.

When she ran it the other way, on a replay she would only ever screen inside her own head, her thumbs moved through a notes app with the tags he dictated under his breath: *14-C, occupancy, diffuser, exhibit*. Cell by cell, the way he did everything. Once he read over her shoulder, close enough that she felt the wool of his charcoal suit at her arm, and told her that her spelling of *municipal* was correct. From Kitsu Kaoru, that counted as fireworks over the harbor.

They moved through rooms that were, legally, not treatment rooms until an inspector said otherwise, which was the entire reason an inspector had come. Capacity forms wanted honest math, and honest math was the one thing a wellness corridor could not diffuse away. Toa counted out loud, the way he had taught her to count steps in the studio: fourteen seats, twelve candles, one diffuser laboring overtime in the corner like it had something to prove.

"Write *diffuser exceeds serenity*," Kaoru said, deadpan, not looking up from his clipboard.

She typed it. "That is not a real citation."

"No. That is flirtation via footnote, To-chan, and I allow it exactly once per inspection." He turned the page. "Now log the candle count under fire load, where it does actual work."

"You called me To-chan in a wellness closet," she said. "Is that witness work or harassment?"

"It is scheduling." He still did not look up. "You respond faster when I use the nickname inventory. I have data on it. You filed three rooms in the time it took the host to find a sprinkler map."

Her phone buzzed against her palm with a calendar ping from the portal, the kind she was not allowed to open mid-inspection, because an open notification was an interruption and interruptions did not belong in the record. She did not need to open it to guess. Something with the word *audit* in the title and harbor water underneath it. Ferry horns. A bed listed somewhere as a clerical error. She turned the phone face-down against her thigh and left it there until Kaoru told her otherwise, and tried very hard not to let her pulse file a comment of its own.

In the treatment-room doorway he stood with his shoulder against the frame, suit crisp, expression doing the work of a locked door so the actual door could stay open for the camera. She stood half a step behind him with the phone raised. Witness posture, not stage posture. He had drilled the distinction into her in a rehearsal studio, chin set where pride could not hide behind it, and she had not expected to need it in a place that sold cucumber water by the carafe. But the body remembers training, and hers held the line.

When he shifted to let a staffer squeeze past, his chest brushed her back. Heat came through tracksuit and wool like the building had a fault in the wall. His mouth came close to her ear, not a kiss, only proximity, only the geometry of two people in a narrow doorway with a job to finish. "Hold still," he said, the same two words as the elevator, in the same low register that did not belong on a municipal feed, and her nipples tightened against her sports bra as if someone had quietly turned the air down ten degrees. She filmed the doorway anyway. Steady frame. *Witness work*, she told her body, in his voice, because his voice was the only thing her body currently obeyed. Her body declined the instruction and filed the moment instead under *Saturday, one bed, clerical error*.

The floor manager intercepted them at aromatherapy storage, the way managers always materialized once the citations started to outnumber the candles. His badge said *Director of Calm* in a serif font that had cost somebody money. Kaoru read the title, declined to comment on it, and told the man he could step aside or join the exhibit list, his choice, both were fine for the record.

Toa had two ways to make herself useful, and the clause covered both. She could stand at the deputy's shoulder and let the hallway become record: firm voice, hands that shook anyway, truth delivered without performance, because performance was the one thing the camera would punish. Or she could defuse it with sponsor charm, promise the man five minutes and a properly posted capacity chart, file patience right beside compliance until he moved aside and the corridor exhaled eucalyptus and defeat.

When she held firm, the hallway went quiet for ten long seconds: rain on glass, the small metal complaints of a clipboard, and a man discovering in real time that *calm* was a job title and not armor. Her voice did not shake. Her hands did. Kaoru did not praise her for the difference. He told her to log the timestamp, because shaking was human and went into the record as human, while falsifying was the other thing, the thing that revoked licenses, and that went into the record too.

After the manager retreated, Kaoru caught her wrist. Not hard. Inventory again, the same way he had taken stock of her in the wrong conference room, cataloguing what the body said that the paperwork could not. "You held firm." His thumb moved once across the inside of her pulse, where the shaking still lived. "Your hands shook. I noticed. I notice things for a living."

"I noticed you noticing," she said. "That is also a skill, you told me. Not an excuse."

Something that was not quite the almost-smile crossed his mouth and then changed its mind. "The harbor weekend will tell us whether you shake on a queen bed or only in corridors." He let go of her wrist, returning it like municipal property. "Do not read romance into that sentence. Read the rider."

Her face went hot in a way the eucalyptus could not explain. "I read the rider twice."

"Good. Read it a third time on the ferry. Riders improve with motion sickness."

When she chose soft instead, on that same private replay, the corridor still filed its defeat, only with less echo to it. The manager's smile died in a professional manner and then resurrected itself as compliance, which was the only afterlife smiles got in this district. Toa heard herself promise patience in a sponsor voice she had not known she owned until the city handed her a reason to use it, warm and certain: five minutes, a posted chart, and everyone keeps their license. Kaoru let her finish, then added a single word, *move*, like a footnote with teeth under everything she had just said politely. The man moved. The eucalyptus exhaled. Nobody thanked anybody, because gratitude on letterhead was still banned, and the break room was a long ferry ride away.

There was no body in the aromatherapy closet. No scandal worth a tram ad, no headline about fraud, none of the television violence Toa had braced for on the walk over. That was the point, and it took her half the morning to recognize it as a point and not an anticlimax. Modern Ryoko Port saved its drama for permits. The danger here was not a knife. The danger was a clause. Toa filmed a mislabeled fire exit, a cluster of candles arranged to be, technically, not seating, and a staff break room where someone's actual lunch smelled like real curry instead of wellness tea, a small honest crime she filed with private affection. Kaoru wrote citations the way other people wrote grocery lists, one line each, no adjectives, every one of them a thread running straight up from his clipboard to the board that could vote a storefront's license closed.

Back at the licensing tower, the inspection closed without blood and without bodies, with three minor citations that would leave the office as polite emails and arrive at Scorpion-grey Holdings LLC as the first soft knock of an enforcement file. Nothing revoked today. Everything noted, for the day it might be.

"You passed witness work," Kaoru said, capping his pen. "That is not a compliment. It is a classification. The office classifies. It does not applaud."

"I will take any classification that keeps my sponsorship active," Toa answered. "Applause is loud and rent is louder."

He did the thing then that she would only later understand the full weight of. He slid an item across her portal, the same municipal portal that held her sponsorship file and his signature on it, and the item was a calendar invite titled like the dullest possible thing: a harbor weekend audit retreat. Ferry slips, Saturday. One bed in an Airbnb listing the filing described as a clerical error, to be corrected only if she asked in writing. *Case 2 with salt air*, she thought, and did not say. He called it a calendar invite, and technically he was right, and technically was the whole city.

What made her stomach drop was not the one bed. It was where the invite lived. Because it went on the portal, it went on the office calendar, and because it went on the office calendar, it went on the Council calendar, which meant a weekend that was mostly going to be his hands and her noise would exist, officially, as continued witness-work and a compliance audit, hours logged, with a line item an auditor or a councilor or, someday, a People & Conduct officer could pull up and read on letterhead. He was not hiding them. He was filing them, in the one place the city could not later pretend it had never seen.

"Accept on the municipal portal," he said, "or argue with the tide. The ferry does not care which one you pick."

She stared at the rider language under the invite, the boilerplate doing its careful work: shared lodging, audit hours, and an explicit, almost defensive negation of romance on letterhead, as if the document were arguing with itself in two fonts. It read like a contract drafted by a man who had once almost smiled at thank-you curry and then filed the memory under *denied* before the smile could reach the record.

"And if I decline?" she asked.

"Then you decline salt air, witness continuity, and my Saturday," he said, in that order, as if he had ranked them and was curious which one would land hardest. "The sponsorship remains. Declining a weekend does not revoke standing. Declining a weekend revokes a weekend. The two are not the same form, and I will not let you confuse them into a yes you did not mean."

That was warmth, in Ryoko Port. Not a confession. A schedule, plus a careful note about which boxes were safe to leave unchecked.

The service elevator remembered them from the wrong-floor building, or she had decided it did, which amounted to the same thing. Rain striped the glass. Their reflections stood a careful municipal distance apart, and then, in the glass, almost touched. This time one of them closed the gap. This time one of them reached.

"Unless the harbor?" he asked.

"That is not a municipal form," she said, echoing the wrong-floor car word for word, and stepped in when he held the door, because she had learned that some queues you joined on purpose.

He badged a floor that did not appear on the public map, a floor that existed only for the service lift and for people who had a reason to be unfindable for ninety seconds. The car shuddered as it dropped. His hand found the small of her back, and she catalogued it the way he had taught her to catalogue everything: bracket, paperwork, lie. She turned her face to the glass and felt him hard against her hip through charcoal wool, and the lie got harder to file.

"You filed incomplete in my elevator," he said, low, against her hair. "Last building. You asked for something and then performed discretion before I could rule on it. The harbor is where you finish the request."

"I asked on the couch," she said. "You counted to three and scheduled later."

"I scheduled Saturday." His mouth found the hinge of her jaw and stayed there, not yet a kiss, the threat of one. "Deliverable negotiation. You will tell me in writing, on the portal, whether one bed is error or invitation. I do not act on implication. I act on filings."

"Invitation," she breathed, before she could perform any discretion at all. "Witness continuity. Audit hours. Not romance on letterhead."

"Correct." He kissed her then, deeper than the couch had dared, tongue and teeth and a patience that felt like an audit of her mouth. One hand slid under her tracksuit at the waist, palm hot on bare skin, and the other gathered her wrists and pinned them, gently, to the brushed-metal rail. She rolled against him and felt him thick and wanting through the wool, and felt him feel it, the small catch in his breath that no spreadsheet ever got to see. "Not here," he said against her mouth. "The harbor has ears too, and I do not intend to deny you twice in the same week. Once was scheduling. Twice would be cruelty, and I bill cruelty differently."

"Deputy Director-sama," she whispered, "that sounds like a promise."

"That sounds like a calendar." He eased back by exact degrees, straightened her collar where his own hand had rucked it, and left her flushed and aching and upright, which she suspected was the part he was proudest of. "Unless the harbor. Not romance on letterhead."

"Unless the harbor," she agreed, legs unsteady under her, her voice steadier than her knees by a margin she hoped the camera in her chest was not logging.

The lift dinged like a stamp coming down on a form she had not seen drafted. Outside, Ryoko Port kept filing rain against windows it owned, patient as an office that never closed. Toa walked out with witness work on her phone, a deputy director's warmth filed under scheduling where no auditor could subpoena it, a weekend the tide would argue with if she forgot to accept it on the portal, and a promise of completion written nowhere official at all, nowhere except her skin, which the Council did not have jurisdiction over yet.

She intended to accept.

On the train back to her rented room she watched the clips again with her headphones in, rain on the window, the gold crane on her chest smeared into the glass reflection like a stamp pressed while the ink was still wet. Deputy Director Kitsu Kaoru had called her witness work a classification and not a compliment. She turned the word over the whole ride and decided she had never been so happy to be filed by anyone.

Floor fourteen B was still the wrong door tomorrow, he had said at the end of the wrong-floor week. She had taken the advice and started reading plates before she opened them. Tonight, though, she read a calendar instead: ferry slips, an audit retreat, one bed described as a clerical error pending her written correction. *The City of Lies*, the tram ad still claimed, peeling at the same corner nobody ever fixed. Maybe. Or maybe cities only lied to you when you let them stamp your gratitude in the wrong place, and she was getting better, room by logged room, at choosing the right one.

She had chosen the witness log. She had chosen the hallway, firm voice and shaking hands and all. She would choose the harbor next, and argue with the tide if the tide insisted, because for the first time since the Crane Conservatory's letter had lied so optimistically about fast tracks, Ryoko Port had given her work that counted out loud, on the record, with her name spelled correctly and her standing intact.

*His side*

The premises license for the Velvet Lantern strip had been sitting in his renewal queue for a month, flagged grey, which in his color system meant *somebody is hiding the owner and hoping I will not read past the first shell*. Scorpion-grey Holdings LLC. He had read past it. He always read past it. That was the unglamorous half of what the Council paid the office to do: not the hearings the city streamed onto the record, but the inspections the city forgot it had authorized, the citations that became enforcement files that became, eventually, a board vote nobody attended.

He could have run the inspection alone. Deputy Saito would have signed off on a solo without blinking. He had put Toa on the witness log instead and told himself it was the clause, because the clause was real and load-bearing and protected them both. A fast sponsorship invited the question of favoritism the moment it cleared the queue. The witness clause answered the question in advance: the artist he had vouched for stood on the record documenting that he ran his inspections straight, no invented dancers, no padded numbers, his signature as signatory of record worth exactly what it claimed. Favoritism insurance. *Failure to attend revokes sponsor standing* was not a sentence he had enjoyed writing. It was simply a true sentence about how the Council worked, and he did not lie on the portal, not even to her, not even when the truth happened to function as a leash.

She had filed three rooms in the time the host took to find a sprinkler map. She had held firm with her hands shaking and had not once tried to hide the shaking, which was the entire difference between a witness and a liability. He had noticed. He had said so. He had then, with considerable administrative discipline, declined to say the rest.

The harbor invite he would defend, if anyone asked, as continued witness-work and a compliance audit, hours logged, line item clean. That was even true. It was also the most honest thing he had done in a year, because he had filed it where the city could see it instead of in the dark: on the portal, on the office calendar, on the Council calendar, where it would sit until someone, someday, pulled the record and read his Saturday on official letterhead. Let them. He had decided he would rather be audited than be a secret. He had not yet decided to say that part out loud, so he filed the decision under *later*, next to the almost-smile, next to the clementines he kept on the armrest and denied noticing, next to every other thing he intended to keep until the port stopped trying to sell it.
