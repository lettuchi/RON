## Prologue · Harbor Glass

Rain did not fall on Ryoko Port so much as file itself against the glass, sheet after sheet, like a clerk who would not be told the window was closed. Toa learned that on the tram in, watching the harbor cranes move through smeared light like punctuation in a sentence no one would read aloud. The Crane Conservatory had sent her off with a transfer letter, a personal stamp she did not yet know how to use, and the kind of optimism that only survives because it has never once met a municipal queue.

The city smelled of diesel and wet concrete and someone's over-sweetened coffee. She had slept in worse places than a hostel bunk three blocks from the licensing tower, but Ryoko Port felt worse than the inland tour circuit in one specific way: everything here wanted a form before it wanted a person. What she needed from the city had a name, a filing number, and a governing body. The name was a performing-arts sponsorship. The body was the Ryoko Port Arts Council, the municipal board that voted on which transfer dancers it would fund, sanction, and allow to legally rehearse, perform, draw a wage, and stay. The number was printed on the packet in her sleeve. And the Council, through its permanent office up in the licensing tower, had so far decided to let hers lapse.

She knew the machinery in outline, the way you know a city from its transit map before you have ridden a single line. The Council was the part that voted, in public, on the record. The office beneath it, the Office of Arts & Licensing, was the part that actually worked: it took your application at a counter, weighed it, and a deputy director there either put his signature on the permit or did not. No signature, no sponsorship. No sponsorship, no Ryoko Port. A transfer dancer without a current permit was, as far as the port was concerned, a tourist with unusually good turnout.

The parking structure under the tower was a cathedral of dripping concrete. Fluorescents buzzed in a key that set her teeth on edge. A tram ad for something called *The City of Lies* peeled at one corner and nobody, apparently, had been licensed to care. Toa stood with her rehearsal jacket zipped to her throat and her tablet sleeve tucked under her arm, watching rain needle the harbor glass beyond the exit ramp. Somewhere below, ferries coughed diesel into the channel. Somewhere above, floor fourteen B waited with a fast track that had not yet learned her name.

A man in a charcoal suit crossed the far lane without looking at her, a badge clipped to his belt, his umbrella unopened because Ryoko Port respected deadlines and not weather. He stepped into the staff elevator with the ease of someone who never rode the public car. She told herself that was not an omen. Omen was a word for stories. This was a city that only wanted her packet date in the correct column.

She rode the public car up with a florist who complained about the cost of a storefront license and a courier who smelled of seaweed. Fourteen B chimed. The doors opened on carpet that was too new for a public intake counter and too quiet for a crowd. Wrong wing, her feet said, before her eyes could confirm it.

She had checked the packet again in the car. Expiration date six weeks gone, circled in red by a clerk who was not present to be argued with. The Conservatory letter insisted Ryoko Port loved its dancers. The calendar insisted otherwise. She straightened her collar anyway. One more elevator, one more corridor, and then she would ask nicely. Very nicely. Asking nicely was a form too, and she had always tested well on forms.

The elevator map said fourteen B. The packet said fast track. Those two facts had not yet shaken hands, and Ryoko Port was not a city that introduced people for free.

## I · Wrong Conference Room

Floor fourteen B, the map promised. Arts Council sponsorship intake. The fast track for performing-arts graduates who could prove, on paper and in advance, that they would never become a headline about fraud.

The municipal licensing tower hummed like a printer that had learned to judge people. Every corridor repeated the same grey carpet, the same badge readers, the same smell of warm toner and the rain tracked in on a hundred pairs of boots. Toa clutched the three things the Conservatory had told her to carry across any bureaucratic threshold: her tablet sleeve, her expired sponsorship packet, and the personal stamp that supposedly made her real. The crane on her jacket was a motif, not a costume. Her ballet flats had soaked through in the parking structure and squelched a small confession with every step.

The elevator did not open on the intake counter. It opened on the wrong wing.

She understood why the instant the doors parted. Fourteen B was split into two halves that did not speak to each other and did not care to. One wing was the public face: Arts Council sponsorship intake, a counter where applicants took a number and waited their turn to slide a packet across the laminate. The other wing was where the packets went to be decided. Glass doors. A badge reader. A plate that did not say Arts Council at all. **Office of Arts & Licensing. Deputy Director.** Intake queued you. Arts & Licensing judged and signed you. She had walked past the queue and straight into the room that issued the verdict.

Inside, a man in a charcoal suit sat before two monitors, his profile sharp as a paper cut, his attention fixed on something on the left screen that had not invited her in.

She stepped forward, because stopping had never once saved her.

"Wrong conference room," he said, without turning. "Again."

Toa heard her own apology arrive half a beat too late, the way it always did when pride and panic tried to share one spine. "Sorry. I'm looking for Arts Council sponsorship intake. The counter."

"The counter is the other wing. You read the floor number and then stopped reading." Now he looked at her, grey eyes taking a fast inventory: wet hair, the expired packet, the stamp case held too tight in both hands. "Reading half a sign and committing to it is a skill. It is not an excuse."

On his left monitor a man in a good tie cleared his throat with the performative patience of elected damage control. The window was a live video call, unmuted, the gallery tiled with faces that had never once danced for a living. It took her a second to place what she was looking at, and then the badge reader and the plate and the two monitors all snapped into the same sentence. This was a public licensing hearing. The Arts Council convened them on the record and streamed them straight onto the municipal feed, because anything the Council did to a permit, granting it, suspending it, revoking it, hanging a rider off it, had to happen where the public could watch and the minutes could catch it. The man in the good tie was not a clerk. He was a councilor, a member of the board that voted. Whatever the gallery typed became part of the record. Whatever the camera caught became, eventually, a clip. The chat scrolled. Someone typed *unprofessional*. Toa felt the word land between her shoulder blades like a sticker she could not reach to peel off.

She could leave and go hunt for a clerk who pointed better. She could knock again like a woman who still believed bureaucracy rewarded good manners. She could wait in the hallway until the building itself took pity on her.

She knocked.

"Third time's the charm," she told the glass. "Or the deputy director. Hopefully both."

Kitsu Kaoru let her in the way a gate lets in weather: as a fact, not a favor.

His nameplate sat beside a mug that had gone cold hours ago and a stack of files tied with the kind of string that meant no one in the office trusted the stapler. Deputy Director, Office of Arts & Licensing. Not elected, the way the councilor on the screen was elected; appointed, permanent, the civil servant who outlasted whichever board happened to be voting that season. Not kind on first inspection. Extremely good at making a room feel like a queue you had already lost.

"Crane Conservatory transfer packet," he said, reading the tablet faster than she could perform humility. "Sponsorship expired six weeks ago. And you walked into a live Council hearing like a pop-up ad."

"The transfer letter said Ryoko Port had a fast track for performing-arts sponsorship."

"Ryoko Port has a queue. The fast track is a lane inside the queue, not a way around it." He gestured at the chair beside the monitor, not the one across the desk facing him. Witness posture, he would call it later. Not stage posture. "You are in the queue now, whether you intended to be or not. Sit there. Out of frame."

The councilor on the screen said her name wrong, two clean syllables flattened into something clerical. Kaoru corrected it without sounding impressed by either of them, and went back to his agenda. Toa sat where she was told and tried not to fidget like a dancer waiting in the wings. Fidgeting was evidence. Evidence was something this city ate for lunch and itemized for dinner.

"Can I at least not be the reason your hearing looks unprofessional?" she asked.

"You already are." His mouth almost did something softer. It thought better of it and went back to neutral. "Sit. Witness posture, not performance posture. I will be auditing both."

He muted the gallery with two keystrokes. It was not an apology; it was housekeeping. "The applicant is present for sponsorship review," he told the councilor, evenly. "The packet error is mine to remediate as signatory. Continue with your agenda item."

The chat lit up the moment the gallery came off mute. *Who is she*, from a ward office. *Hair like a press release*, from someone who used emoji in municipal threads. Kaoru typed one line into the record without glancing at her: *Witness only. Do not engage.*

Toa kept her hands flat on her knees and her eyes on the edge of his monitor, where the rain smeared the window behind it into grey. He ran the hearing the way another man might run a spreadsheet: every cell filled, every objection logged in the order received, every flare of sympathy redirected to a form number before it could spread.

"Deputy Director," the councilor said, "your applicant is blocking the agenda."

"My applicant is seated where I placed her, and she has not said a word." Kaoru's voice did not rise; it simply closed, like a drawer. "If the board's agenda cannot tolerate one silent witness in the corner of one office, the board should revise the agenda."

Someone laughed in a tile and muted themselves before the laugh could be entered into the minutes. Kaoru unmuted no one. He slid a sticky note across the edge of the desk toward her without looking down. Block letters. *Do not speak unless I prompt you. If I prompt you, answer in dates and names only. Nothing the chat can quote.*

She nodded once. He turned back to the camera.

"Packet expiration," the councilor tried again.

"Remediated, pending studio proof and the applicant's choice of rider," Kaoru said. "You will have the signed PDF on the record tonight."

"When tonight?"

"When I file it." He clicked a cell shut. "Next item."

When the councilor asked whether performing-arts intake had been the one to send her up, Kaoru did not pass the blame down the hall to a clerk. "Intake sent her to the wrong floor," he said. "For the purposes of this packet, I am intake now."

That was not comfort. It was jurisdiction. And she discovered, sitting wet in a chair that had not been meant for her, that she preferred jurisdiction to kindness, because kindness in Ryoko Port always arrived with an expiration date stamped on the back.

When the hearing finally ended and the gallery blinked out tile by tile, he did not offer her praise. He offered her a studio key and a hallway that smelled of floor wax and rain.

## II · Witness Work

The Office of Arts & Licensing rented the rehearsal room out by the hour to applicants who needed to prove, in a mirror, that they were something more than a packet error. A mirror wall. A barre. Rain ticking the glass like a clerk tapping the flat of a stamp against an ink pad. Toa loved rooms like this, because they told the truth about bodies and could not be bribed. You could lie to a councilor for a whole hearing. You could not lie to a mirror for eight bars.

"So you want me to audition," she said. "For a permit extension."

"I want proof that you can follow choreography without flirting with the clerks who renew you." He set his tablet on a folding chair and propped it like a lectern. A compliance checklist was open on the screen, not a critic's notebook. There was no applause anywhere in his face. "Run the phrase."

She ran it once. The apology lived in her shoulders, where it always hid. He counted under his breath, low and even, the way a man counts witnesses through a doorway and not the way an audience counts down to applause.

"Again. Less apology in the port de bras."

She ran it again. Cleaner. Angrier, a little, which helped more than she wanted to admit.

"That one was clean," she said, before she could stop herself. "You counted out loud."

"Counting is witness work." He closed the sponsorship draft on the tablet with one thumb. "It is half of what your permit will ask of you. Stop expecting applause from a licensing officer. You will only embarrass us both."

He marked something, then surprised them both by adding to it. "I will say one thing for the record and deny it under oath later. You stopped apologizing on the second pass. That is discipline, not charm. Charm I can manage with two keystrokes and a mute button. Discipline is the only thing in this building I respect without a form ordering me to." He looked back down before the sentence could finish landing on his own face. "Again. From the top."

He made her run the entrance twice more, then the turn, then the exit with her chin lifted to where pride could not duck behind it. Sweat gathered at her hairline and darkened the crane on her jacket. His suit did not wrinkle. His voice stayed level the whole hour, a metronome that happened to wear wool.

Between passes he marked the tablet without complimenting her: a checkmark here, a checkmark there, then a deliberate pause on the phrase where her wrist had softened. "Again," he said. "Your sponsor will not forgive a soft wrist on the day someone decides the story is fraud."

"I am not fraud."

"I did not say you were. I said the story." He let that settle. "My signature is what stands between you and the story. When I sign a sponsorship, I am the signatory of record, which means I personally vouch to the Council that you meet the standard and that you will not, on any given Tuesday, embarrass the seal that funds you. So you will not dance like a graduate hoping for flowers. You will dance like a witness who can survive cross-examination, because a witness who can survive cross-examination is exactly what I have agreed to swear you are."

He tapped the screen and turned it toward her. "Which brings us to the part you walked in early for. There are two riders that can hang off the sponsorship I sign for you. Read both before you romanticize anything, elevators included."

She read. The standard performance rider was the plain one: discretion, attendance at scheduled reviews, no posts tagging municipal accounts, no city seal cropped into a meme. The exclusive rehearsal sponsor rider was longer and quieter and made her throat go tight for reasons that had nothing to do with the dry language. After-hours studio access. Direct deputy review. Calendar integration. Folded into both riders was a witness clause, the same one his counting had been drilling into her all afternoon: as a fast-tracked applicant, she would be required to attend his compliance inspections as a documenting witness. Favoritism insurance, the rider called it, in those words. Proof on the public record that the deputy who signed her was not inventing dancers or playing favorites with the ones he licensed.

The terms were procedural. They still made her breath catch, because *calendar integration* read, to a body that had spent the afternoon counting in time with his voice, like his hand at the small of her back before she had earned it. And lower, if she let her mind follow where it had already wandered once in the mirror.

"I can follow a calendar," she said.

"Posture improves the moment you stop auditioning for forgiveness," he answered, which was not the same thing, and was somehow an answer anyway. "File that under things you already knew."

"I file better when I know which box I'm checking."

He almost looked directly at her then. Almost. "The exclusive rehearsal sponsor rider is not a romance clause. I want that on the record between us before anything is on the record anywhere else. It is after-hours access and direct deputy review. It is my calendar. It is not dating on letterhead."

"Say what that means," she said. "*On letterhead.* You keep using it like a wall, and I want to know which side of the wall I'm standing on."

He set the stylus down, which from him was the equivalent of another man loosening his collar. "On letterhead would mean I filed us. Your name beside mine in a box, a relationship of record, a thing the Council can open and read and rule on, the way it rules on a permit or a grievance. The rider is the opposite of that. Calendar. Clause. Audit. After-hours access. Everything kept procedural, so that neither of us ever has to write the true word anywhere a clerk could subpoena it. We do not keep it off the record because it is small. We keep it off the record because it is the one thing I am not prepared to let the port hold over either of us."

She heard the shape of it land, and laughed once, not unkindly. "So that is the trick. We file the courtship as procedure precisely so we never have to admit it is a courtship. The clause *is* the flirting. The audit *is* the date. *Not romance on letterhead* is just the most elaborate way two people have ever found to not say a word out loud." She tipped her head. "I will not put that in writing either. I am learning. The realest things go in the drawer with no label."

"You learn quickly," he said, which was as close as Kitsu Kaoru came, that afternoon, to agreeing.

Underneath both riders the thing itself was simple enough to say in one breath: the Ryoko Port Arts Council authorized her to work in the port as a performing artist, and his name on the permit was the Council's proof that she would not make them regret it. The sponsorship was the permission. The riders only decided how close the deputy who granted it would have to stand while she used it.

She picked exclusive, because the fast track had already lied to her once, and she was done being polite about distance.

"You understand that this clause puts you on my calendar," Kaoru said, one more time, as if repetition were its own consent form. "Not romance on letterhead."

"I understand paperwork," Toa said. "I am very, very good at paperwork, when someone bothers to explain the boxes."

He held her gaze one beat longer than a licensing officer strictly should. "Then read every box twice," he said, "before you sign."

## III · Unless?

The building hour passed six. The public elevators sighed into standby one by one, the way an office exhales when the last applicant is gone. The service lift still answered when he badged it, a dull metal box that did not appear on the floor map tourists photographed in the lobby.

He held the door and looked at her the way he had looked at the expired date on her packet: problem, opportunity, item to file.

"Unless?"

"That is not a municipal form," she said.

"The contract floor is closed for the night. This floor is not on your calendar yet. Get in."

Rain striped the glass on the way down. The car shuddered once, as if the building had cleared its throat at the two of them. His badge tapped the reader beside a floor that did not appear on the public map at all, the way some hours did not appear on any schedule a clerk could pull. Harbor lights slid past the seams in the doors like lines someone had redacted before filing.

What happened next stayed filed under sponsorship in every way that mattered to an auditor, which was the only way that mattered to him. She did not perform purity about it afterward. She learned where his calendar hid the hours no clerk would ever be allowed to review, and she learned how his voice could drop to something that was not cruel, only low and close enough to feel like permission.

"Quiet," he said, to the reflection in the glass rather than to her directly. "You wanted the exclusive rehearsal rider. The exclusive rehearsal rider means I audit deliverables."

"Then audit quietly, Deputy Director-sama. The building still has ears, and most of them file reports."

He huffed something that might have been amusement, if amusement had ever been one of the duties listed under his job title.

His hand found the small of her back, not a caress so much as a bracket, the kind he might draw around a figure that needed checking, and it pulled her into the heat of him. Paperwork bracket, her mind insisted, primly, even as her hips disagreed and leaned. She turned her face to the rain-streaked panel and felt his breath at her ear. The contract language was still there between them, a third person in the car who had not been invited and had no intention of leaving.

"Deliverable one," he murmured. "You hold still when I count."

"I can hold still."

"Deliverable two." His other hand traced the line of her jaw, thumb finding the corner of her mouth, then pressing in until her lips parted around it. "You do not perform gratitude on glass."

"I file my gratitude in the break room. You said so yourself."

"Good." He kissed her then, and after the first contact it was not brief at all: exact, hungry, his tongue finding hers with the same unhurried patience he gave a contested cell on a spreadsheet. She answered him, because her body had been counting in time with him all afternoon and because the elevator was the only room in the whole tower that did not want a witness other than him. She tasted coffee gone cold and rain, and she wanted considerably more than taste.

He lifted her heel to the barre bolted along the service wall, a joke only a dancer would catch, and she laughed once, surprised out of herself, before his mouth found her throat and the laugh turned into a sound she did not recognize as hers. His suit jacket folded itself over the rail with more care than he was giving anything else. Her rehearsal jacket followed it. The gold stripes bunched at her waist while his palm slid under the hem of her tracksuit top, skin to skin, and she arched into the contact without a single count of choreography to tell her to.

"Say stop if you mean it," he said against her collarbone.

"I mean keep auditing."

His thigh pressed between hers, denim and wool and the hard line of him she could feel even through the layers. She rolled her hips once, shameless about it, and heard him exhale like a man who had just lost a motion he had fully intended to win. His fingers found the waistband of her leggings and stopped there, asking the question without words. She nodded against his mouth. He did not strip her. He worked two fingers under the fabric and found her wet, swollen, ready, and she bit down on his shoulder to keep quiet when he began to stroke her with that same merciless evenness, counting under his breath again, as if her body were one more thing he could bring into compliance on tempo.

"Deliverable three," he said, his voice gone rough at the edges. "You do not come on municipal property unless I say so."

"Deputy Director-sama," she breathed, "that is cruel."

"That is scheduling." He kissed her until her knees gave up their argument, and then he eased his hand away before she could crest. "Incomplete filing. You finish on my calendar, not on a whim in a service lift."

She wanted to argue. She wanted to grind against him until the whole building heard the verdict. She stayed still instead, because stillness was also desire when he was the one commanding it, and because the denial left her throbbing, obedient, furious, and lit all the way up.

He lifted her higher on the barre bolt and ground against her through his trousers, once, twice, a promise rather than a completion. She felt him hard and hot against her belly and chased the friction until he caught both her wrists and pinned them, gently, to the glass.

"Not tonight," he said. "Not on glass, where the next badge could call the car. You will ask in writing for the rest."

"I will ask in writing," she promised, and she meant it with her whole body.

When she trembled, he steadied her with a palm at her hip. "Breathe," he said. "That is acceptable noise."

After, he straightened his tie without bothering to check the mirror, as if the mirror were a witness he had not authorized. She fixed her hair with fingers that still shook a little and pulled her leggings back up with the taste of his mouth still on hers and the ache of almost still sitting between her thighs like an unresolved line item.

"File it under exclusive rider," he said.

"Already filed," she answered.

The car rose. The building pretended nothing at all had happened. That, too, was Ryoko Port: the truth behind the glass, and the paperwork out in front of it.

*His side*

The exclusive rehearsal rider was a risk cell he had colored red in his own spreadsheet weeks ago and then left blank, the way a man leaves one chair at the table, until someone worth the exposure walked through the wrong door. As signatory of record he carried every name he signed; the exclusive rider doubled the weight, because it put his calendar where the Council could subpoena it. He had left it blank on purpose. He had filled it anyway.

Pop-up ad was accurate. Crane Conservatory hair, expired dates, a stamp case held like a weapon she did not yet know how to use. He had almost muted the entire hearing and sent her down to the intake counter where she belonged. Instead he had filed her beside the monitor, out of frame, and watched the public chat try to eat her alive on the municipal record.

*Unprofessional* was cheap. *Who is she* was cheaper. He typed *Witness only* and meant *Mine to remediate*, which was worse, and more honest, and the kind of thing a signatory was not supposed to feel about an applicant whose permit he held.

The studio proved what the packet could not: spine first, the moment she stopped apologizing with her shoulders. He counted out loud because counting kept his hands busy and kept his voice out of the register that would have turned a leaked clip viral by morning and handed the board a reason to pull her file.

Unless was not a municipal form. It was the word that had slipped past him once in the canon prologue of some other life he did not admit to remembering. Service lift. Rain. One floor that lived off the public map. He audited deliverables because audit was the only verb his job title allowed for the thing he actually meant, which was wanting.

When she said *Deputy Director-sama* against the glass and rolled her hips into his thigh like a woman who had finally stopped auditioning for forgiveness, the spreadsheet that lived where other men kept a conscience noted the data and filed it: applicant responds to structure, and to denial, in roughly equal measure. Wet on his fingers. Stop word offered, unused. Do not offer her romance on letterhead. Offer the calendar. Offer the blanket as a loan. Withhold the almost-smile until the port stops trying to sell the one thing he intended to keep off the market. Deny completion in the car. Save the rest for a couch, after hours, and a clause she would sign twice and mean both times.

He adjusted his tie. He closed the elevator access log before it could timestamp anything inconvenient. He wrote *after-hours rehearsal* in the cell, the way the rider permitted, and locked the sheet.

## IV · Official Lentils

The next midday she cooked thank-you curry in the municipal break room of the Office of Arts & Licensing and committed the grave administrative sin of bringing her personal stamp to a paper plate.

"They don't give out case numbers upstairs for a pot of curry," she told the roux while it thickened. "So today I am cooking gratitude, not filing a report about it. There is no form for this. I checked."

Kaoru arrived with his tie loosened and his skepticism fully intact. "The break room is not a hearing room," he said. "Neither is the lid of that pot, no matter how officially you are stirring it."

"I brought the stamp, though. A proper hanko makes a thing official, does it not? You sign permits with yours. I am simply applying the principle more broadly."

"A deputy's chop on a sponsorship makes an artist legal in this port. The same chop on a paper plate makes lunch." He took the plate she offered, looked down at the red stamp impression she had centered on disposable cardboard like a small parody of his entire authority, and then, against what looked like his own better judgment, sat down. "Put my chop on paper, Toa. Not on your tableware."

He ate without praise and without flinching, as though his jurisdiction had quietly expanded to cover broth. Toa watched his chopsticks and tried very hard not to hope too visibly, because hope, like fidgeting, was evidence.

"The queue has a long memory for applicants who treat the seal like confetti," he said, when the bowl was empty.

"I promise to never again stamp confetti, or curry, or anything the Council has not pre-approved."

"See that you don't."

The fluorescents hummed their one note. Rain kept filing itself against the window in even sheets. Somewhere upstairs a printer jammed and someone swore at it with the low tenderness of a prayer. Toa washed the pot while he rinsed his cup, their shoulders almost touching at the narrow sink. His sleeve brushed her wrist. She did not flinch away from it. She leaned into the contact instead, as if it were witness work, and let him feel that she had done it on purpose.

"You counted in the studio," she said, carefully, watching the suds and not his face. "You counted all the way through. You did not count to the end in the elevator."

"Those were different deliverables."

"Motion to compel an explanation?"

"Motion denied." His hand settled at her waist, two fingers only, the same place he had bracketed her in the car. "Your contract clears in three business days. Until it does, do not perform gratitude on municipal letterhead. Perform patience. Patience is the only thing the record cannot hold against either of us."

She turned her face up toward his. He looked at her mouth, then at the pot, then deliberately away. "Patience is also a form," she said.

"Then file it," he answered, and took his hand back as though the fabric had burned him.

## V · Three Business Days

The sponsorship contract came back to her tablet sleeve with a fresh effective date and his signature already locked at the foot of it, the deputy's chop that turned a lapsed applicant back into a working artist the port would let stay. Three business days until it took effect. Not a love letter. Binding all the same, which was arguably more than a love letter could claim. She read the effective line twice, because numbers were safer to look at than whatever it was that lived in her chest when he stood too close in a small metal room.

He walked her to the glass doors of his office wing, the Arts & Licensing side, not the couch yet, as if a few feet of distance could reclassify the whole afternoon into something the record would approve of. Harbor lights smeared across the window beyond the glass. Inside, on the armrest of the couch, a small pile of clementines waited, bright as little suns he would absolutely deny having bought.

The sponsorship tablet chimed: effective, pending her signature. She signed with a hand that still remembered the exact pressure of his fingers under her waistband. He watched the stylus and not her face until the date locked into the Council's system. Then he stood close enough that she felt the heat of him through the wool, and the elevator's unfinished deliverable announced itself again, low in her belly, like an item carried forward to the next meeting.

"Three business days," he said. "Do not perform gratitude on municipal letterhead."

"Filed," she answered. "And I will be asking, in writing, for the part you denied me on glass."

His gaze dropped to her mouth. "Bring that request to the couch," he said. "Not to the elevator access log. One of those I can keep off the record."

After hours, on the office couch, he loaned her a blanket against her noise. The harbor lights smeared across the window. She took the far cushion. He took the near one. The blanket became a shared blanket the moment she shivered and he pulled it over both of them without a word about it, the way he did everything he did not intend to put in writing.

"The blanket is a loan against your noise," he said. "Snore, and I reassign you to the public waiting area with the rest of the intake queue."

"Then I will file my breathing under acceptable noise and renew it nightly."

"Peel one," he said, nodding at the clementines on the armrest. "Domestic filing. I will deny that too, right up until the day the port stops trying to sell the one thing I intend to keep."

She peeled. The citrus smell cut bright through the toner and the rain. He set his tablet on the armrest and reviewed tomorrow's queue with one hand, the deputy still on duty even off the clock, while the other hand stayed under the blanket, palm up, an invitation he had filed as an accident so that neither of them would have to call it what it was.

She laid her knee over his thigh. He did not move away. "Deliverable four," she murmured. "Couch. After hours. Still sponsorship."

"Still sponsorship," he agreed, and he kissed her with the peel still curled in her fingers, slow and deep, the kind of kiss that made her forget municipal letterhead entirely, until his hand slid up the outside of her tracksuit thigh and stopped exactly where the elevator had stopped. Denying again. Teasing again.

She rocked against his palm through the fabric and heard herself whimper into his collar. He counted to three, unhurried, against her ear, and then he lifted his hand away. "Ask in writing," he said, his voice wrecked enough at the edges to satisfy her that the denial was costing him something too.

"I am asking now," she said. "With my body. Consider it a verbal motion."

"Accepted." He pulled her into his lap, the blanket tenting over them both, and this time he let her grind down against him until she came, quiet and shaking, against his trousers, her face buried in his collar, his grip on her hips the only permission she needed and the only one she wanted. He held her through the aftershocks. He did not take his own release. That, he filed for later. That, too, was scheduling.

"You almost smiled," she tried, when she could speak again, "when I said thank-you curry." Courage, in her experience, always showed up wearing her stupidest shoes.

"Motion denied on the smiling." His thumb traced her lower lip. "Motion granted on staying until you breathe normally."

"Good night, Deputy Director-sama."

"Good night, To-chan."

He paused at the door on his way out. "Floor fourteen B is still the wrong door tomorrow morning," he said. "Try reading the whole plate before you open it this time. Intake is the other wing."

She fully intended to. She also fully intended to come back, queue and all, wrong door and all, because Ryoko Port had finally handed her a room with a mirror that did not lie and a man who counted witness work out loud and would not, under any agenda item, admit that he liked the sound.

Outside, the rain kept filing itself against the glass. Toa peeled a second clementine, one she had probably stolen fair and square, and thought about exclusive riders, paper plates, and the difference between a permit and the whole life that grew up around one once you stopped apologizing with your shoulders.

The city of lies, someone had called it on a tram ad she had never bothered to read all the way through. Maybe. Or maybe cities only lied to you when you let them stamp your gratitude in the wrong place, on the wrong plate, in the wrong wing.

She would choose the paper next time. And the couch. And the door she meant to read before she opened it.

But not tonight.
