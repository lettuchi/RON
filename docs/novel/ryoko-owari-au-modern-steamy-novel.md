## One · Harbor Glass

Rain did not fall on Ryoko Port so much as file itself against the glass, sheet after sheet, like a clerk who would not be told the window was closed. Kakita Toa learned that on the tram in, watching the harbor cranes move through smeared light like punctuation in a sentence no one would read aloud, holding three things the Crane Conservatory had sworn would be enough: a transfer letter, a personal stamp she did not yet know how to use, and a body that had never once been allowed to want anything it couldn't choreograph.

The tram smelled of wet umbrellas and other people's breakfasts. A schoolboy slept against the window with his bag clutched to his chest like a permit he expected someone to contest. Across from her, two office workers compared lanyards in low voices, and Toa caught herself reading the badges before the faces, which the city had taught her to do in under seventy-two hours. In Ryoko Port the badge was the face. The face was just the thing the badge wore to meetings.

She had been in the city three days. It had rained for all of them — not dramatically, never dramatically, nothing in this port committed to drama when drizzle could be logged at lower cost. The rain simply arrived each morning like a clerk reporting for shift, took up its position against every window in the municipal district, and worked.

The Conservatory had sent her off with optimism, which was the one document nobody at the other end would stamp.

She kept replaying the send-off, the way you keep pressing a bruise to confirm it still holds. Madame Akodo's office had smelled of rosin and chrysanthemum tea, and the old woman had laid the transfer letter on the desk between them with two fingers, the way she laid out everything she considered both precious and insufficient.

"Ryoko Port loves its dancers," Madame had said. "The letter says so. Letters lie at a higher standard than people, which means they lie better. You will go anyway."

"Yes, Madame."

"Three rules." Madame Akodo held up a finger as crooked as a cane. "One: the apology lives in your shoulders. Evict it. It pays no rent and it ruins your line. Two: in a government building, ask nicely. Asking nicely is a form too, and you have always tested well on forms." The second finger. Then the third, slower. "Three: when the port offers you a door, read the whole plate before you open it. The half you skip is always the half with your name on it."

Then she had pushed a lacquered stamp case across the desk — black, gold crane on the lid, the personal hanko inside carved with Toa's name in characters she had practiced signing since she was nine. "You are a graduate now. The stamp makes you real to a city like that one. Try to be real to yourself first. It is cheaper, and no office can revoke it."

Toa had cried on the inland train exactly once, between stations, where no schedule could log it. Then she had straightened her shoulders, evicted the apology for the first of what would prove to be several hundred times, and read the transfer letter again until the words stopped meaning anything except *go*.

The letter promised a fast track. The calendar disagreed. Her performing-arts sponsorship had expired six weeks ago — circled in red by a clerk who was not present to be argued with — and a transfer dancer without a current permit was, as far as the port was concerned, a tourist with unusually good turnout. She knew the machinery in outline, the way you know a city from its transit map before you have ridden a single line. The Ryoko Port Arts Council was the board that voted, in public, on the record: which transfer dancers it would fund, sanction, and allow to legally rehearse, perform, draw a wage, and stay. Beneath the board sat the office that actually worked, the Office of Arts & Licensing, where applications were weighed and a deputy director either put his signature on the permit or did not. No signature, no sponsorship. No sponsorship, no Ryoko Port.

What the letter had not sent along was anything a port city actually traded in. No patron waiting at the tram. No company director who owed the Conservatory a favor. No aunt in the ward office, no ex-classmate on the Council payroll. Ryoko Port was a city of introductions, and she had none. Just talent, paperwork, and a permit date the calendar had already crossed out.

The hostel was three blocks from the licensing tower, eight bunks to a room, lockers that took exact change and grudges. Her bunkmates were a ferry courier who smelled permanently of seaweed and rope, and a barista named Emi who had opinions about the municipal government the way other people had hobbies.

"Arts Council?" Emi had said, on night one, hanging upside down off the top bunk like a bat with a nose ring. "Good luck. My cousin waited four months for a busking permit. Four months. For a *ukulele*. By the time it cleared, she'd moved inland and taken up ceramics. The ceramics permit took five."

"I have a fast track," Toa had said.

Emi had laughed so hard the bunk frame filed a complaint.

Days one and two had gone the way Emi predicted and the letter did not. Day one: the ward office, where a kind woman with a cardigan and the eyes of a hostage told her that performing-arts sponsorship was not, in fact, a ward matter, and had not been since the consolidation, and would she like a pamphlet about the consolidation. The pamphlet was eleven pages. Toa read all eleven on a bench under an awning while the rain filed itself against the street, because not finishing things was a habit she had never been able to afford.

Day two: the licensing tower's ground-floor information desk, where a man with a lanyard and no discernible pulse had glanced at her packet, circled nothing, fixed nothing, and said the words *fourteen B* with the intonation of a man releasing a fish too small to keep. The intake queue on fourteen B had been ninety strong by the time she found it. She had taken a number. The number had outlived the business day. A clerk had appeared at four-forty-five, surveyed the survivors with genuine municipal sorrow, and suggested they all try again tomorrow, earlier, or possibly in a previous life.

That night Sango had called from the inland circuit, voice bright over the rattle of a tour bus.

*how's the port. is it glamorous. are you famous yet*

*it rains like the rain is salaried,* Toa typed. *my permit expired six weeks ago and the queue ate my whole day and the fast track is a rumor.*

*okay but have you cried*

*once. on the train. unlogged.*

*good girl. tomorrow go straight to the top floor and be charming at someone important. you're a Conservatory graduate, not a queue number.*

*charm is not a form, Sango.*

*EVERYTHING is a form to you. this is why you're single.*

Toa had put the phone face-down on her chest and stared at the bunk slats above her and listened to the courier snore in maritime rhythms, and thought: the packet has a number. The Council has a name. The deputy director has a signature. Somewhere in that tower is the one pen that makes me legal, and tomorrow I am going to stand in front of it and ask nicely, because asking nicely is a form, and I have always tested well on forms.

The third morning, she dressed in the black rehearsal tracksuit with the gold stripes down the legs and the gold crane stamped over her heart — a motif, not a costume, the last thing the Conservatory's costume mistress had pressed into her hands — because every other outfit in her bag still apologized in the shoulders, and Madame's first rule was the only one she had managed to keep so far. Her ballet flats had not yet dried from yesterday. They squelched a small confession with every step.

The licensing tower stood over the municipal district like a filing cabinet that had won a war: forty floors of grey glass, rain running down its face in long disciplined columns, a lobby you could land a small aircraft in if the aircraft had an appointment. The tram ad on the shelter outside was for something called *The City of Lies* — a show, a perfume, a prestige drama, she never found out — peeling at one corner, and nobody, apparently, had been licensed to care.

She came in through the parking structure because the rain had escalated from filing to litigation and the structure was the only covered route from the tram stop. It was a cathedral of dripping concrete. Fluorescents buzzed in a key that set her teeth on edge, the exact pitch of an interval her body wanted to resolve and the building refused to. Somewhere below, ferries coughed diesel into the channel. Somewhere above, floor fourteen B waited with a fast track that had not yet learned her name.

That was when the man in the charcoal suit crossed the far lane.

He did not look at her. That was the first thing she noticed, and she noticed it because everyone else in three days had looked at her — at the wet hair, the tracksuit, the packet clutched like a boarding pass for a flight that had left — with the brief diagnostic pity a port reserves for the unprocessed. This man's attention was a beam with no scatter. Badge clipped to his belt. Umbrella unopened, carried like an afterthought, because Ryoko Port respected deadlines and not weather. He moved through the dripping concrete the way a signature moves across a page: once, cleanly, without apparent effort, leaving something binding behind.

He stepped into the staff elevator with the ease of someone who never rode the public car, and the doors closed on him like a folder.

Something low in her noticed the line of his shoulders before her brain could file an objection. She filed the objection late, the way she would later learn to file everything about him: after the fact, in the wrong drawer, under a label that fooled no one.

She told herself that was not an omen. Omen was a word for stories. This was a city that only wanted her packet date in the correct column.

The public elevator arrived in its own time, which was the only time it had. She rode up with a florist complaining into her phone about the cost of a storefront license — "renewal went up nine percent, NINE, for the same four walls" — and a courier who smelled of the same seaweed as her bunkmate, possibly fraternally. The florist's argument had numbers in it. Everyone's arguments here had numbers in them. Toa was beginning to understand that in Ryoko Port, a feeling without a number attached was not admissible, and a number without a form behind it was just a feeling that had dressed up.

She checked the packet again in the car, although she had checked it at the hostel, and on the tram, and in the parking structure, the ritual checking of a document that did not change. Expiration date six weeks gone, circled in red. The Conservatory letter, insisting in serif font that Ryoko Port loved its dancers. The calendar insisted otherwise, and the calendar, she was learning, outranked love in every jurisdiction this city maintained.

Fourteen B chimed.

The doors opened on carpet that was too new for a public intake counter and too quiet for a crowd of ninety. Wrong wing, her feet said, before her eyes could confirm it. The intake queue, the counter, the laminate, the take-a-number machine with its tongue of paper — all of it was somewhere else, behind her, on the other side of the floor. What stood in front of her was a corridor with glass doors at the end of it, a badge reader glowing its small red refusal, and a plate beside the doors that did not say Arts Council at all.

**Office of Arts & Licensing. Deputy Director.**

She read half the plate. She would admit that later, under cross-examination, on a couch, with citrus on her fingers: she read *Arts*, and she read *Licensing*, and her feet — wet, tired of queues, three days into a city that wanted a form before it wanted a person — committed to the half she had read.

Madame's third rule knocked politely somewhere at the back of her skull. *Read the whole plate before you open the door.*

The elevator map said fourteen B. The packet said fast track. Those two facts had not yet shaken hands, and Ryoko Port was not a city that introduced people for free.

Toa straightened her collar, evicted the apology from her shoulders, and walked toward the wrong door on purpose-adjacent momentum — and the door, which should have been locked, which was always locked, clicked open under her hand on a badge reader's half-second of mercy as someone exited the far side of the wing, because even a tower of forty grey floors contains, somewhere in its circuitry, one moment of weather.

She stepped through it.

Behind her, the rain kept filing itself against the harbor glass, sheet after patient sheet, like a clerk who knew, better than anyone in the building, that the window was about to be extremely open.

She had no idea, yet, that she was in a story. She thought she was in a queue.

In Ryoko Port, she would learn, there was no meaningful difference — except that a queue ends, and the best stories simply change floors.

## Two · The Wrong Conference Room

Floor fourteen B was split into two wings that did not speak to each other and did not care to. One wing was the public face: Arts Council sponsorship intake, a counter where applicants took a number and waited their turn to slide a packet across the laminate. The other wing was where the packets went to be decided. Toa understood the architecture the instant she was inside it, the way a dancer understands a stage from the wings: out there was the house, and back here was the place where the casting happened, and she had just walked through the pass door without a lanyard.

The corridor hummed. The whole building hummed, she would learn — like a printer that had learned to judge people — but back here the hum had a lower, more confident register, the sound of machinery that no longer needed to advertise itself. The carpet swallowed her squelching flats apologetically. Doors stood at intervals, each with its small plate, each plate a complete sentence she was now, belatedly, reading in full. *Records.* *Compliance.* *Hearings — Authorized Personnel.* And at the end, glass double doors standing one inch open on someone's exit, and beyond them, a corner office with rain down two walls of windows, and a man at a desk before two monitors, his profile sharp as a paper cut.

The man from the parking structure. The charcoal suit. The unopened umbrella, hooked now over a coat stand like a punctuation mark waiting for a sentence bad enough to deserve it.

She stepped forward, because stopping had never once saved her.

"Wrong conference room," he said, without turning. "Again."

The *again* landed strangely — intimate, like an inside joke she had not been present for the start of. Later she would learn that the wing's badge reader failed open on an average of twice a quarter, that lost applicants washed up against his glass doors the way driftwood washed up against the breakwater, and that he logged each one in a spreadsheet titled, without affection, NAVIGATION FAILURES, FY CURRENT. Later still, much later, she would learn that her row in that spreadsheet had been annotated. But in the moment, all she heard was a voice pitched low and even, a voice that did not need volume because it had jurisdiction instead.

Her own apology arrived half a beat too late, the way it always did when pride and panic tried to share one spine. "Sorry. I'm looking for Arts Council sponsorship intake. The counter."

"The counter is the other wing. You read the floor number and then stopped reading." Now he turned, and the grey eyes took a fast inventory — wet hair, the expired packet, the stamp case held too tight in both hands, the gold crane over her heart — and she felt the inventory like a hand drawn slowly down her spine, cataloguing vertebrae, noting deficiencies, lingering nowhere and missing nothing. "Reading half a sign and committing to it is a skill. It is not an excuse."

"It was a confident half," she said, before her better judgment could file a motion. "I committed fully."

Something at the corner of his mouth considered existing and decided against it. He turned back to the monitors.

The left one, she saw now, was a live video call — and it took her a second to assemble what she was looking at, and then the badge reader and the door plates and the two monitors all snapped into one sentence. A public licensing hearing. The Arts Council convened them on the record and streamed them straight onto the municipal feed, because anything the Council did to a permit — granting it, suspending it, revoking it, hanging a rider off it — had to happen where the public could watch and the minutes could catch it. The gallery was tiled with faces that had never once danced for a living. The man in the good tie at the center of the grid was not a clerk; he was a councilor, an elected member of the board that voted, currently performing patience the way other men performed surgery: visibly, and expecting applause.

The chat scrolled down the margin of the feed. Someone from a ward office account typed *who let that in.* Someone with an emoji in their municipal handle typed *hair like a press release.* And then, from the councilor's own staff account, the word that found the gap between her shoulder blades and stuck there like a sticker she could not reach to peel off: *unprofessional.*

Toa stood in the doorway of the wrong conference room, dripping on authorized carpet, six weeks expired, on camera in a hearing she had not known existed ninety seconds ago, and ran the options the way she ran choreography — fast, full-body, committed. She could retreat to the hallway and hunt for a clerk who pointed better. She could wait for the building to take pity, which, on three days' evidence, the building had no department for. She could knock like a woman who still believed bureaucracy rewarded good manners.

She knocked.

"Third time's the charm," she told the glass. "Or the deputy director. Hopefully both."

Kitsu Kaoru let her in the way a gate lets in weather: as a fact, not a favor. His nameplate sat beside a mug that had gone cold hours ago and a stack of files tied with string, which meant no one in this office trusted the stapler. *Deputy Director, Office of Arts & Licensing.* Not elected, like the tie on the screen — appointed, permanent, the civil servant who outlasted whichever board happened to be voting that season. He held out one hand for her packet without ceremony, and read it faster than she could perform humility.

"Crane Conservatory transfer. Graduate track, honors notation, choreographic supplement." A flick of the page. "Sponsorship expired six weeks ago. And you walked into a live Council hearing like a pop-up ad."

"The transfer letter said Ryoko Port had a fast track for performing-arts sponsorship."

"Ryoko Port has a queue. The fast track is a lane inside the queue, not a way around it." He gestured at the chair beside the monitor — beside, not across; the chair that shared his side of the desk, angled out of the camera's eye. "You are in the queue now, whether you intended to be or not. Sit there. Out of frame. Witness posture, not performance posture. I will be auditing both."

"What's the difference?"

"Performance posture asks to be looked at. Witness posture can survive being looked at. You will need the second one in this building considerably more often than the first." He was already turning back to the feed. "Sit."

She sat. The chair was still warm from no one; it had the untouched feel of furniture kept for a contingency the office hoped never to need. Up close, the desk resolved into a system: files in tied stacks by some gradient she couldn't parse, three pens in parallel, a single sticky-note pad squared to the corner of the blotter like it had been arrested there. No photographs. No plants. One drawer of the credenza behind him stood a half-inch open, and from it came, improbably, the faint bright smell of citrus.

On screen, the councilor cleared his throat with the performative patience of elected damage control. "Deputy Director, before we resume — for the minutes — your office appears to have acquired an audience."

"The applicant is present for sponsorship review," Kaoru said, evenly, to the camera. "The packet error is mine to remediate as signatory. Continue with your agenda item."

"The minutes will want a name."

"The minutes may have one. Kakita Toa, Crane Conservatory transfer." The councilor tried the name and flattened it, two clean syllables ironed into something clerical. Kaoru corrected the pronunciation without sounding impressed by either of them and went back to his agenda.

The agenda, Toa learned over the next forty minutes of enforced stillness, was the city itself, fed through a grid one cell at a time. A venue license appeal from a jazz room on the wharf that had failed an egress inspection twice and was attempting, per the councilor, *to litigate the width of its own corridor.* A busking-permit backlog item, table for table's sake, that made her think of Emi's cousin and her abandoned ukulele. A storefront renewal schedule with a nine-percent fee adjustment — the florist from the elevator, Toa realized, was somewhere underneath that line item, arguing with a phone in a stairwell — and, threaded through all of it, the hostile weather of the chat, which had not finished with her.

*who is she,* asked the ward office account.

*intake is THAT way lol,* offered the emoji handle.

*unprofessional,* the councilor's staffer typed again, in case the minutes had missed it the first time.

Kaoru muted the gallery with two keystrokes. It was not chivalry; it was housekeeping — she understood that even then, and was obscurely grateful that it wasn't kindness, because kindness would have meant she owed something, and she had nothing current to pay with. Then he typed one line into the public record without glancing at her: *Witness only. Do not engage.*

The chat, denied, turned on the jazz room instead.

She watched him run the hearing the way another man might run a spreadsheet: every cell filled, every objection logged in the order received, every flare of sympathy redirected to a form number before it could spread. His voice never rose and never hurried. It was a voice that had renounced every register except the one that could be quoted safely in minutes, and the discipline of it was doing something to her that she filed, at the time, under *nerves*. It was not nerves. Her body, conservatory-trained, had simply recognized a metronome of unusual quality and begun, without permission, to keep his time. Her breath had settled into the cadence of his sentences. Her pulse had found his tempo and matched it. She sat in the wet chair learning the rhythm of a stranger's authority by heart, and told herself it was professional interest, and her body, which had heard every lie she ever told it, let her keep this one for now.

"Deputy Director," the councilor said, somewhere past the half hour, "your applicant is blocking the agenda."

"My applicant is seated where I placed her, and she has not said a word." Kaoru's voice did not rise; it simply closed, like a drawer. "If the board's agenda cannot tolerate one silent witness in the corner of one office, the board should revise the agenda."

Someone laughed in a tile and muted themselves before the laugh could be entered into the minutes. The councilor's jaw performed a recalculation. Toa sat very still and felt the sentence go through her like a current — not the content of it, the *shape*: a man spending exactly as much force as the obstacle required and not one watt more, on her behalf, without once looking at her.

He slid a sticky note across the edge of the desk toward her without looking down. Block letters, drafted at some point she had entirely failed to observe. *Do not speak unless I prompt you. If I prompt you, answer in dates and names only. Nothing the chat can quote.*

She nodded once. He returned to the camera.

"Packet expiration," the councilor tried again, with the air of a man reaching for the last weapon in the drawer.

"Remediated, pending studio proof and the applicant's choice of rider," Kaoru said. "You will have the signed PDF on the record tonight."

"When tonight?"

"When I file it." He clicked a cell shut. "Next item."

And when the councilor asked, silkily, whether performing-arts intake had been the office to send her up — fishing for a clerk to feed to the minutes — Kaoru did not pass the blame down the hall. "Intake sent her to the wrong floor," he said. "For the purposes of this packet, I am intake now."

That was not comfort. It was jurisdiction. And Toa discovered, sitting wet in a chair that had not been meant for her, watching a stranger absorb her error into his own remit like a line item he had decided to own, that she preferred jurisdiction to kindness — because kindness in Ryoko Port always arrived with an expiration date stamped on the back, and jurisdiction, apparently, arrived with a man who said *I am intake now* in a voice that her pulse had been keeping time with for three-quarters of an hour.

The hearing ground through its remaining cells. The jazz room lost its appeal and was granted thirty days to widen its corridor or its ambitions. The busking backlog was tabled with honors. The fee adjustment passed, nine percent, and somewhere in a stairwell a florist's afternoon got worse. Then the councilor read the closing formula at the speed of a man late for a better meeting, and the gallery began to blink out, tile by tile, faces vanishing into the city like fish released — too small, all of them, to keep.

The office went quiet. Rain filed itself against two walls of glass. Kaoru looked at the monitors for a moment longer than the dead feed required, the look of a man confirming a door had truly latched, and then he turned the chair a quarter and regarded her: the full attention this time, no camera to share it with, and she sat up under it before she had decided to.

"You held still," he said. "Forty-one minutes. Most navigation failures last four before they start explaining themselves."

"You counted."

"Counting is most of my job. The rest is signatures." He set her packet on the blotter between them and squared it to the edge, and the small precision of the gesture made it, somehow, the most serious thing anyone had done with her name in six weeks. "Here is your position, Kakita Toa, stated once, so we do not waste each other's afternoon. Your sponsorship is six weeks lapsed. Intake can queue you, and the queue is four months deep, and your hostel budget is not." A glance at her flats, which had confessed everything. "A deputy director can remediate a lapsed packet on his own signature, today, if and only if the applicant can prove in a studio that the talent the packet claims actually exists. I do not sign stories. I sign witnesses."

"You're offering me an audition."

"I am offering you an appointment. Four o'clock. The office rents a rehearsal room two floors down — mirror, barre, no audience, no applause. Bring the body the Conservatory says you have." He opened the squared packet, made one mark inside it, and closed it again. "If the studio confirms the paperwork, we discuss riders. If it does not, the counter is the other wing, and the queue will teach you what it teaches everyone."

"Why?" The question got out before she could dress it. "You could have had security walk me down forty minutes ago. The chat wanted you to."

He stood — unhurried, final, a man reassembling himself toward the next item on a long agenda — and took from his desk drawer a key on a plain municipal fob, and set it on the blotter where the packet had been. The citrus smell ghosted up from the credenza drawer and vanished.

"The chat wants a story," he said. "I want the queue to mean something, which requires that talent be processed on its merits even when it arrives wet, late, and through the wrong door. Those are not the same appetite. Four o'clock. Studio twelve. If you are early, warm up. If you are late, do not come."

He did not offer her praise. He did not offer her sympathy, or a towel, or the smallest measurable unit of charm. He offered her a studio key and a hallway that smelled of floor wax and rain, and went back to his monitors before she had fully risen from the witness chair, as if she were a cell he had filled correctly and the grid had already moved on.

Toa walked back down the corridor of complete sentences with the key pressed into her palm hard enough to leave the shape of its teeth, past *Hearings* and *Compliance* and *Records*, through the glass doors, into the elevator lobby where the public car waited with its doors open like a held breath. Her flats squelched. Her pulse, she noticed, was still keeping his time.

*how did it go,* Sango texted, with three lanterns and a fish.

Toa looked at the key for a long moment.

*I got an appointment,* she typed. *Four o'clock. With the man who is intake now.*

*that sentence makes no sense*

*neither does this city. it's possible I love it here.*

## Three · Witness Work

She had three hours to fill and a city that charged by the hour to fill them. She spent the first in a noodle shop across from the tower, ordering the cheapest bowl on the menu and eating a third of it, because her stomach had reorganized itself around four o'clock and was accepting no other appointments. The cook watched her push noodles around the bowl with the resigned expression of a man who had fed a thousand applicants on hearing days and could read a docket in a person's chopsticks.

"Tower?" he said, finally.

"Tower."

"Permit or grievance?"

"Audition."

He considered this, wiped the counter, and put a second fish cake in her bowl without comment or charge. It was the first unfiled kindness Ryoko Port had extended her, and she nearly cried into the broth, which would have been, in this city, a reportable dilution.

Studio twelve lived two floors below the licensing wing, down a corridor that smelled of floor wax and rain. The key turned like it had been waiting. Inside: a mirror wall, a barre bolted at two heights, a grey resilient floor scarred by a decade of other people's proof, and rain ticking the glass like a clerk tapping the flat of a stamp against an ink pad, patient, regular, certain of eventual use. The room told the truth about bodies and could not be bribed. That was the thing about studios that no hearing room would ever have: you could lie to a councilor for a whole afternoon. You could not lie to a mirror for eight bars.

Toa loved rooms like this the way other people loved hometowns.

She was early, so she warmed up, because he had said *if you are early, warm up,* and because her body would have demanded it regardless of any deputy's instructions. She gave herself a full barre out of habit and superstition both — pliés first, the knees confessing yesterday's hostel bunk; tendus, the wet flats traded for proper shoes; rond de jambe, hip sockets unlocking one degree at a time; the old order, the inherited liturgy, slow before fast and small before large. In the mirror, the apology was visible in her shoulders even now, even alone. Six weeks of expiration had moved in up there and started receiving mail.

*Evict it,* said Madame Akodo, from four hundred kilometers inland. *It pays no rent and it ruins your line.*

At 3:58, the door opened.

Kitsu Kaoru entered the way he seemed to enter everything: punctually, completely, and without transitional weather. He had come down in the same charcoal suit, carrying the tablet and a folding chair which he placed at the exact midpoint of the mirror wall — the seat a répétiteur takes, she noted, not the seat an audience takes — and propped the tablet on it like a lectern. The screen, she could see in the mirror, showed a compliance checklist where a critic's notebook should be. Boxes. Fields. A drop-down menu, presumably with no option for *luminous*.

"So you want me to audition," she said. "For a permit extension."

"I want proof that you can follow choreography without flirting with the clerks who renew you." He uncapped the stylus. No applause anywhere in his face, and none pending. "The packet claims a Conservatory graduate with honors notation and a choreographic supplement. The packet is paper. You are the exhibit. The exhibit moves; the paper does not; when the two disagree, I sign the exhibit." He nodded at the floor. "Your entrance phrase. The one from your graduation piece, which is listed in the supplement. Run it."

He had read the supplement. He had read all eleven pages of it, she realized, in whatever interval his afternoon had allowed between her error and her appointment, the way she had read the consolidation pamphlet on a wet bench: completely, because not finishing things was a habit neither of them could afford.

She took the corner. Rolled her shoulders once. Found first position, and the room got very quiet underneath the rain.

She ran it once. The phrase was sixteen bars: an entrance from upstage in a low diagonal, a développé held against the body's own impatience, a turn sequence that resolved into a long arabesque, the exit dissolving into bourrées like a sentence trailing off on purpose. She had performed it for the Conservatory's entire faculty and an examiner from the capital. She performed it now for one municipal official and a folding chair, and the apology rode her shoulders the whole way through, where it always hid, where every photograph of her dancing since the expiration had it on file.

He counted under his breath while she moved. Low and even, almost inaudible, *one, two, three* threaded under the phrase like a watermark — and the counting got under her skin in a way she elected not to examine. It was not how ballet masters counted; they counted at you, ahead of you, dragging the room toward the music. He counted the way a man counts witnesses through a doorway, the way a man counts a deposition's exhibits: each number a confirmation of something already true. Her body started keeping his time anyway. Hips finding the pulse of his voice like it had been written into the choreography all along, breath dropping into the spaces between his numbers. By the final bourrée she was dancing to him instead of to the absent music, and she did not entirely notice, which was how it always began with things her body decided before her.

She finished. Held. Released.

He made a mark. "Again. Less apology in the port de bras."

Not *beautiful*. Not *thank you*. Not even *good*. She stood there one second longer than professionalism allowed, recalibrating around a man who gave corrections like citations — specific, actionable, devoid of cruelty and praise alike — and then something in her chest shifted gears with an almost audible clunk. Fine. *Fine.* If the room only spoke compliance, she would dance compliance fluent.

She ran it again. Cleaner. Angrier, a little, which helped more than she wanted to admit; anger had always been better scaffolding than hope. The développé hit its full height and stayed there an extra count out of pure spite. The arabesque drew its line all the way out through her fingertips to the rain.

"That one was clean," she said, coming out of it, breath high. "You counted out loud."

"Counting is witness work." He marked the tablet. "It is half of what your permit will ask of you. Stop expecting applause from a licensing officer. You will only embarrass us both."

"Do you ever applaud?"

"I once initialed a margin twice." He did not look up. "The auditor in question still mentions it. Again, from the top. And this time the wrist."

"What about the wrist?"

"It went soft in the turn sequence. Sixth bar. Your supplement says the turn is the centerpiece of the phrase; a soft wrist in a centerpiece is a discrepancy, and discrepancies are what audits eat." He finally looked up, and the grey eyes had the same fast-inventory quality as before, except now the inventory was technical, joint by joint, and somehow that was worse, or better; she declined to adjudicate. "Your sponsor will not forgive a soft wrist on the day someone decides the story is fraud."

"I am not fraud."

"I did not say you were. I said the story." He set the stylus down across the tablet, a small ceremony of full attention. "Understand what you are actually asking me for, because the queue upstairs does not understand it, and that is why the queue is four months long. When I sign a sponsorship, I am the signatory of record. I personally vouch to the Council that you meet the standard — that you will not, on any given Tuesday, embarrass the seal that funds you. Every artist I have ever signed is a sentence in my file, not just hers. If one of them is ever found fraudulent, lazy, or invented, every signature I have ever made is re-examined in that light. So you will not dance like a graduate hoping for flowers. You will dance like a witness who can survive cross-examination, because a witness who can survive cross-examination is exactly what I am considering swearing you are."

The rain ticked. The mirror held them both, the seated man in charcoal and the dancer breathing hard in the center of the floor, and Toa thought: *no one has ever described believing in me as a liability they were choosing to underwrite, and I am horrified to discover what it does to my pulse.*

"From the top," he said, retrieving the stylus. "Wrist."

She gave him the wrist. Then he wanted the entrance again — "you arrive like you are hoping the floor will not notice; arrive like the floor was filed in advance" — and then the turn in isolation, four times, his count laid under her like rail, and then the exit with her chin lifted to where pride could not duck behind it. Sweat gathered at her hairline and darkened the crane on her jacket. Her thighs began the low harmonic burn of real work. His suit did not wrinkle. His voice stayed level the whole hour, a metronome that happened to wear wool, and by the fifth pass she had stopped performing for him and started working with him, which was a different transaction entirely, and the room knew it before she did.

Somewhere in the sixth pass, he surprised her.

"New material," he said. "I will dictate; you will translate. Consider it a comprehension audit. Glissade. Jeté ordinaire. Pas de bourrée under, fourth, pirouette en dehors, double, land fourth, hold two counts. Then the arabesque from your phrase, but facing the mirror, not the door."

She blinked. "You read music," she said slowly, "and apparently you read ballet."

"I read files. Your art form has the decency to publish its vocabulary. Most of the bodies I audit are buildings; the terminology for those is worse, and nothing in a building ever lands fourth." A beat. "Can you do it or not?"

"I can do it." She marked it once with her hands, the dancer's shorthand, his sequence flickering through her fingers — and then she did it full out, glissade jeté, the little traveling burst, pas de bourrée melting under her, the double pirouette snapping around to land clean in fourth, two counts, *one, two*, in his voice, in her head, and out into the long arabesque with the mirror taking her line and giving it back without commentary.

Silence. Rain. Her own blood in her ears.

"Why facing the mirror?" she asked, still in it, arm extended toward her own reflection.

"Because the door is where audiences come from. The mirror is where you come from." He made a mark. "An artist who can only be honest toward the door is a performance. The Council funds witnesses. Come out of it before your standing leg files a grievance."

She came out of it. Walked a small circle, hands on hips, breathing. He allowed the water break without being asked, which from him was practically a fruit basket, and she drank at the barre and watched him in the mirror while he reviewed the checklist — the deliberate way he moved through the fields, no box skipped, the cold mug of his attention now entirely full of her, in writing. The flush on her chest was cardio. Most of it was cardio. She was a professional with a professional's relationship to her own heart rate and she could identify, with clinical precision, the percentage that was not cardio, and she elected to round it down and file the difference.

"I will say one thing for the record," he said, eyes on the tablet, "and deny it under oath later. You stopped apologizing on the second pass. That is discipline, not charm. Charm I can manage with two keystrokes and a mute button. Discipline is the only thing in this building I respect without a form ordering me to."

"And off the record?"

"There is no off the record in this building." He looked up. "There is only the drawer with no label, and you have not earned the drawer."

*Yet* sat at the end of that sentence, unfiled, and they both heard it, and neither of them entered it into the minutes.

"Final pass," he said. "Full phrase, plus the dictated material as a coda. I count you in; after that the counting is yours. A sponsor is not always in the room. The standard is."

He counted her in — four beats, low, even, and gone — and she danced the whole assembled thing into the rain-light, entrance and développé and the turn with its honest wrist, glissade jeté, the double, fourth, two counts of her own counting now, alone on her own rail, and out through the long arabesque to the mirror, to herself, to whatever it was she had carried four hundred kilometers in a wet duffel and nearly let six weeks of red ink convince her wasn't there.

She finished. The room held its one note. He looked at her for three full seconds, which she would later learn was, by his standards, an ovation.

Then he made the last mark, capped the stylus, and stood. "The exhibit and the paper agree," he said. "I will remediate the packet tonight. Upstairs, before the building closes, there is one more document — the sponsorship carries a choice of riders, and you will read both before you sign anything, because the riders decide considerably more than the sponsorship does. Fifteen minutes. Do not cool down into a puddle."

He folded the chair, returned it to its corner with geometric finality, and paused — one fractional pause, his hand on the door — as if some appendix to the afternoon had not yet been ruled on.

"The fish cake place across the street," he said, not turning around. "The cook reports to no one. If you are going to eat a third of a bowl before auditions, eat the third with protein in it."

The door closed on whatever her face did.

He did not owe her the signature. The queue was full of applicants with better connections and packets that hadn't lapsed, and a deputy who vouched for talent the Council had not yet met was spending his own credibility on a wet stranger from the wrong conference room. The bureaucratic lifeline was not kindness. It was jurisdiction with a chop on it — jurisdiction that had noticed, in passing, between hearings, what she ate.

She found, watching the door he had gone through, that she wanted both. The chop and the noticing. The signature and the man who counted.

She had fifteen minutes, and a puddle was forming anyway, and none of it was cool-down.

## Four · Two Riders

The licensing wing after five o'clock was a different organism. The phones had stopped. The corridor of complete sentences stood in low light, every plate legible, every door closed, and through the glass walls the city had gone to dusk — harbor lights coming on in strings, rain still filing, the towers across the district lit in the patchwork of late workers and forgotten switches. Toa came up from the studio with her hair damp at the temples and her legs humming and her whole nervous system still running on the rail of his count, and found the glass doors of the deputy director's wing unlocked for her, which was its own kind of sentence.

He was at the desk. Of course he was at the desk; she suspected the desk would have to be decommissioned by the board before he would leave it ahead of the work. The cold mug had been replaced by a colder one. The tied stacks had rotated like crops. He glanced up exactly long enough to confirm she was the scheduled interruption rather than an unscheduled one, and indicated the witness chair, which had somehow already migrated back to the camera-blind side of the desk, as if it remembered her.

"The packet is remediated," he said, without preamble. "Studio proof attached, my certification drafted. The sponsorship itself is simple enough to say in one breath, so I will say it once: the Ryoko Port Arts Council authorizes you to work in this port as a performing artist, and my chop on the form is the Council's proof that you will not make them regret it. You may rehearse, perform, contract, and be paid. You may stay. That part is decided; I decided it two floors down, somewhere around the wrist."

Her breath went somewhere and came back. Six weeks of red ink, three days of queues, four hundred kilometers — resolved in a subordinate clause, between agenda items, by a man reading from a tablet. She had imagined this moment involving more trumpets. The trumpets, she was discovering, were a thing she was going to have to learn to hear inside the paperwork.

"Which brings us to the part you walked in early for." He turned the tablet toward her. "There are two riders that can hang off the sponsorship I sign for you. The riders do not decide whether you stay. They decide how you and the deputy who signed you are allowed to occupy the same building afterward. Read both before you romanticize anything. Elevators included."

"Why would I romanticize an elevator?"

"No reason," he said, in the tone of a man entering a future exhibit into evidence, and pushed the tablet the last inch toward her.

She read. She read the way he had taught her to dance that afternoon — completely, no boxes skipped — and the documents rewarded the discipline, because the difference between them lived deep in the boilerplate where a skimmer would never find it.

The standard performance rider was the distant one. Clause by clause it assembled a life: scheduled compliance reviews during business hours, conducted by a rotating auditor from the office pool. Discretion regarding municipal accounts. No city seal cropped into a meme, which was apparently a clause they had needed to write down at some point, for someone, and Toa wanted that story very much. The witness clause sat folded into it like a load-bearing wall — as a fast-tracked applicant she would attend the signing deputy's inspections as documenting witness, proof on the public record that the man who vouched for her ran his inspections straight. *Favoritism insurance*, the rider called it, in those words. The fine print was blunt about the price of absence: *failure to attend revokes sponsor standing.* Under the standard rider, the queue would remember her as an artist who filed boring paperwork and kept her distance. Proximity professional. Independence intact. A rotating auditor — "Deputy Saito, most quarters," Kaoru said when she asked, "competent, punctual, no opinions about wrists" — would learn her compliance and never her coffee order.

The exclusive rehearsal sponsor rider was the close one.

After-hours studio access, when the public rooms went dark and the building belonged to the badge readers. Direct deputy review instead of the rotating pool — every deliverable, every renewal, every inspection log crossing one desk, his. And calendar integration, which was bureaucratese for his schedule and hers sharing cells the intake counter never saw: her rehearsal blocks on his grid, his inspection blocks on hers, two names recurring in adjacent rows for as long as the sponsorship ran. The same witness clause. The same favoritism insurance — heavier, if anything, because under exclusive review the inspections and the rehearsals and the logged hours all piled up beside one name, and the one name was his.

One rider kept her at arm's length from the man whose signature let her stay. The other put her on his calendar and in his after-hours rooms. Both kept her legal. Only one decided whether distance was a comfort or a cost.

The terms were procedural. They still made her breath catch, because *calendar integration* read, to a body that had spent the afternoon counting in time with his voice, like his hand at the small of her back before she had earned it. And lower, if she let her mind follow where it had already wandered once in the mirror, somewhere around the sixth pass, when his count had laid itself under her hips like rail and she had stopped being able to tell whether she was moving on the music or on him.

She made herself read the exclusive rider a second time, beginning to end, because wanting a document was exactly the condition under which a document most needed re-reading. The clauses did not change. Her pulse declined to change either.

"Questions," he said. Not *any questions?* — an instruction.

"Direct deputy review. In practice."

"In practice: when your studio proof is due, I take it in person rather than assigning it out. When your renewal is contested — renewals are always eventually contested; this is a port — the contest lands on my desk with my own certification already in the file, which means I defend my own judgment rather than a stranger's. Faster for you. Heavier for me." He said *heavier for me* the way other men said *partly cloudy*.

"And the witness clause. I document your inspections. Why does a man who muted a councilor mid-hearing need insurance?"

"Because I muted a councilor mid-hearing." He leaned back fractionally, the chair conceding a centimeter. "A fast sponsorship invites exactly one question: why her, why so quickly, what does the deputy get. The clause answers it in advance, on the record, in the only currency the record accepts — your presence at my inspections, logging that I do the job straight. You are not insurance because you are suspected. You are insurance because I am, perpetually, by design. Every signatory is. It is what the signature is *for*."

"And if I miss one?"

"Then the insurance lapses and the question reopens and your standing goes with it. The clause is not decorative, Kakita Toa. Nothing in this office is decorative. The office does not have the budget."

She looked at the two documents side by side on the screen, and then at him over the top of them. The lamp put a warm edge on the charcoal wool. Behind him the harbor was strings of light in falling water, and the citrus ghost came and went from the credenza drawer, unexplained, unfiled.

"The exclusive rider is not a romance clause," he said, watching her read him instead of the riders. "I want that on the record between us before anything is on the record anywhere else. It is after-hours access and direct deputy review. It is my calendar. It is not dating on letterhead."

"Say what that means. *On letterhead.* You keep using it like a wall, and I want to know which side of the wall I'm standing on."

He set the stylus down, which from him, she was learning, was the equivalent of another man loosening his collar. For a moment he looked past her at the rain, assembling the sentence the way she had watched him assemble everything all day: completely, before committing any of it to delivery.

"On letterhead would mean I filed us," he said. "Your name beside mine in a box. A relationship of record — a thing the Council can open, read, and rule on, the way it rules on a permit or a grievance. Reviewed at the board's pleasure. Quoted in minutes. Held over you at your first contested renewal and over me at my next promotion docket; produced, cropped, captioned. That is what the letterhead does to the things written on it: it makes them the city's." His eyes came back to her. "The rider is the opposite instrument. Calendar. Clause. Audit. Everything kept procedural, so that neither of us ever has to write the true word anywhere a clerk could subpoena it. Understand me precisely, because I will say this part once. We do not keep it off the record because it is small. We keep it off the record because it is the one thing I am not prepared to let the port hold over either of us."

The office was very quiet. The rain filed one full page of the window.

*It.* He had said *it* like the word had a case number, like *it* was already a live file with exhibits accruing, and the most clerical pronoun in the language sat there in the middle of his sentence glowing like a struck match. She heard the shape of what he had actually disclosed — drafted in negatives, executed in boilerplate, deniable to any board in the city — and laughed once, not unkindly, and felt the laugh turn warm halfway through, because what he had just described, in the driest language ever drafted by a municipal hand, was wanting her. Carefully. In advance. With provisions.

"So that is the trick," she said. "We file the courtship as procedure precisely so we never have to admit it is a courtship. The clause *is* the flirting. The audit *is* the date. *Not romance on letterhead* is just the most elaborate way two people have ever found to not say a word out loud." She tipped her head, studying him the way she studied a combination before marking it. "I will not put that in writing either. I am learning. The realest things go in the drawer with no label."

"You learn quickly," he said, which was as close as Kitsu Kaoru came, that afternoon, to agreeing.

She thought about it. She made herself think about it, properly, because Sango's voice in her head was already shrieking *the close one, obviously, oh my GOD* and decisions made by Sango's voice had a documented failure rate. She thought about Deputy Saito, competent and punctual, learning her compliance and never her coffee order; about business hours and rotating pools and a clean professional distance from the one person in this city who had read all eleven pages of her. Distance was a comfort. Distance was the thing the Conservatory had drilled into her about patrons and boards and men with signatures: keep the wall, keep the wall, the wall keeps you.

But the fast track had already lied to her once. The letter had promised a lane that did not exist, and the city had eaten three of her days and most of her nerve, and the only thing in Ryoko Port that had turned out to be exactly what it claimed was the man across this desk, who promised nothing, certified everything, and counted out loud. She was done being polite about distance. Politeness about distance was just apology wearing a longer coat.

"Exclusive," she said.

"Read every box twice before you—"

"I read it twice. I read the standard one twice too. I can recite the meme clause." She held out her hand for the stylus. "Exclusive."

He gave her the stylus. She initialed the witness clause and the calendar cells both, pulse loud in her ears the whole way down the page — *T.K., T.K., T.K.*, her own initials marching down the margin like a corps de ballet — and then the document wanted the thing she had carried four hundred kilometers and never once used.

The stamp.

She took the lacquered case out of her jacket. Opened it. The hanko sat in its bed of red silk beside the little pot of vermilion paste, her name carved in mirror-script on the stone face, and her hand — which had not shaken through one hearing, one audition, or one full hour of his counting — shook now.

"I've never used it," she admitted. "On anything real."

"Then the first impression will be the legal one. Fitting." He came around the desk — the first time all day he had crossed onto her side of any room — and stood beside her chair, close enough that the wool of his sleeve held the day's last warmth near her cheek. "Ink the face evenly. Edge to edge; the paste does not forgive favoritism either. Then place it in the box, weight it straight down — no rotation, rotation smears the name — hold for one count, and lift clean."

"One of your counts or a normal one?"

"Mine," he said. "Obviously."

She inked the face, edge to edge. Set the stone to the screen's signature plate where the office's hybrid form wanted its physical impression, felt his attention settle over her hand like a second hand without weight, and pressed. *One* — his tempo, unhurried, the length of a held breath in a service doorway. Lift, clean.

Her name stood in the box in vermilion, crisp at every edge, real.

Kakita Toa, sponsored performing artist, Ryoko Port. Effective in three business days.

"You understand that this clause puts you on my calendar," he said, one more time, level, from very near, as if repetition were its own consent form — and at this range she could hear what the levelness cost, a thin discipline over something with considerably more weather in it. "Not romance on letterhead."

"I understand paperwork," Toa said, looking up at him with the stamp still warm in her fist. "I am very, very good at paperwork, when someone bothers to explain the boxes."

He held her gaze one beat longer than a licensing officer strictly should — long enough that the room went small and warm around the two of them, long enough that she felt it travel all the way down, past every initialed clause, to somewhere no rider had jurisdiction. Then he straightened, retrieved the tablet, and was the Deputy Director again at one stroke, the file closing over the moment like water.

"The signed PDF goes to the Council record tonight, as promised," he said. "The councilor will be disappointed by how boring it is. That is the optimal outcome with councilors." He paused, squaring the tablet to the blotter. "Your calendar access activates with the contract. You will find the integration... thorough."

"Is that a warning?"

"It is a feature description." He did not look up. "Good evening, Kakita Toa. The public elevator is to the left. The other one is not on your calendar yet."

She was halfway down the corridor of complete sentences before the word *yet* finished detonating, in stages, like weather coming in off the harbor. She typed three messages to Sango in the elevator and sent none of them, because there were no dates and names that covered it, and everything else was poetry, and she had been advised about poetry.

The rain had downgraded to mist. She walked the three blocks to the hostel with her stamp case in her fist and her name in vermilion on a municipal record and a clause in her future shaped exactly like a man's calendar, and she thought: *the wall keeps you, Madame said. But nobody at the Conservatory ever explained what happens when the wall has a door in it, and the door has a plate, and you have finally learned to read the whole plate — and you walk in anyway.*

Three business days.

She was going to need a better tracksuit.

## Five · Unless

The rider activated on a Wednesday, quietly, the way everything official happened in Ryoko Port: a notification on her tablet at 9:00 a.m. precisely, a new badge permission, and a calendar that was suddenly, vertiginously, not only hers.

She opened the integration on the tram and nearly missed her stop. There it was — the shared grid, exactly as drafted: her name in its own color threaded through the office's week, rehearsal blocks she had not yet booked sitting as pale suggested cells, and his schedule running beside hers in disciplined columns. Inspections. Hearings. A recurring Thursday block labeled only *QUEUE*, two hours, inviolable, like a man scheduling an appointment with the tide. She could see when Deputy Director Kitsu Kaoru ate lunch, which was apparently *never*, and when he left the building, which was apparently *later than the building*. Calendar integration. Thorough, he had said. A feature description.

She booked studio twelve for that evening, after hours, because the rider said she could, and because some clauses you test the way you test a new pointe shoe: immediately, before respect for the object can set in.

The building at night was a different animal again — past six, the public floors went down like stage lights, section by section, and what remained was the badge-reader economy: cleaners, deadline cases, the one illuminated wing on fourteen where a cold mug was presumably being replaced by a colder one. Studio twelve answered her new permission with a click that felt ceremonial. She warmed up alone in the mirror with the harbor lights for an audience and worked the graduation phrase for an hour, then the dictated coda, then her own material — the choreography she had not shown anyone, the supplement to the supplement — because the room was honest and the building was empty and her permit was real, and for the first time in seven weeks her body belonged entirely to her own account.

He appeared at the door at 7:40, jacket on, no tablet.

She saw him in the mirror first — a charcoal interruption in the doorframe, watching not like an audience and not like an auditor but like a man who had found an unscheduled item in a room he was responsible for — and she finished the phrase before acknowledging him, because finishing things was a habit, and because letting him watch was, she could no longer pretend otherwise, a pleasure with a low flashpoint.

"Direct deputy review," she said, to his reflection. "Is this it? Already?"

"This is a man confirming the badge system works before he leaves." He stepped inside, let the door close. "The review is Friday. On the calendar. Which you have read, since you booked this room through it at 9:04 this morning."

"9:03."

"9:04. The portal logs to the second; do not negotiate with infrastructure." He crossed to the barre, set one hand on it, and looked at the last of her phrase still hanging in the room the way smoke hangs after a match. "That material is not in your supplement."

"It's mine. Unfiled." She was still breathing high; she let him see it. "The realest things go in the drawer with no label. I had it on good authority."

Something moved behind the grey — inventory again, but slower, and this time the inventory was not technical, and they both stood in the knowledge of it while the rain ticked the dark glass like a clerk who had stayed late on purpose.

"The building hour passed six some time ago," he said at last. "I will walk you down."

The public elevators had sighed into standby one by one; the lobby lights beyond the corridor stood at their night setting. But the service lift still answered when he badged it — a dull metal box that did not appear on the floor map tourists photographed in the lobby, doors opening on brushed steel and a barre bolted along the back wall, of all things, some retired fixture from a renovation nobody had filed the disposal forms for.

He held the door and looked at her the way he had looked at the expired date on her packet: problem, opportunity, item to file.

"Unless?"

"That is not a municipal form," she said.

"The contract floor is closed for the night. This floor is not on your calendar yet. Get in."

She got in.

The doors closed with the soft authority of a verdict. Rain striped the glass panel on the descent. His badge tapped the reader beside a floor that did not appear on the public map at all, the way some hours did not appear on any schedule a clerk could pull, and the car began to move through the building's dark interior like a thought the tower was declining to finish out loud. Harbor lights slid past the seams in the doors like lines someone had redacted before filing.

The car was small and warm and entirely too quiet. The mirror-dim panel gave her both of them at once: the dancer with her jacket zipped to the throat and her pulse misbehaving at the wrist, and the deputy director standing at the regulation distance with his hands at his sides — except that the regulation distance, she realized, had been shrinking since the lobby button, by degrees too small to cite, the way a tide comes in: no single wave responsible, the water simply nearer each time you look.

"You watched me for four minutes before I acknowledged you," she said, to his reflection, because the reflection felt like the version of him it was legal to address. "In the studio. I count too, you know. It's half of my job."

"Three minutes, fifty seconds. The portal logs—"

"—to the second. Do not negotiate with infrastructure." She turned her head, and the reflection-version of him was abandoned mid-sentence, because the real one was much closer than the regulation distance, and the heat coming off the wool of him crossed the gap before anything else did. "What did the review conclude, Deputy Director-sama? Off the calendar. The unfiled material. The drawer with no label."

"The review concluded," he said, in the low register the chat would never hear and the minutes could never hold, "that the exhibit considerably exceeds the paper."

She had been keeping his count in her body since a hearing room in the rain — five days of it now, tempo lodged under her hips like a stone in a shoe — and the count had nowhere left to go.

"Quiet," he said, to her reflection in the glass rather than to her directly. "You wanted the exclusive rehearsal rider. The exclusive rehearsal rider means I audit deliverables."

"Then audit quietly, Deputy Director-sama. The building still has ears, and most of them file reports."

His hand found the small of her back — not a caress so much as a bracket, the kind he might draw around a figure that needed checking — and it drew her into the heat of him. *Paperwork bracket*, her mind insisted, primly, even as her hips disagreed and leaned. She turned her face to the rain-streaked panel and felt his breath at her ear, slow and even, a man with all the time in the queue.

"Deliverable one," he murmured. "You hold still when I count."

"I can hold still."

"Deliverable two. You do not perform gratitude on glass."

"I file my gratitude in the break room. You said so yourself."

"Good." He kissed her then, and after the first contact it was not brief at all: exact, hungry, unhurried, the same patience he gave a contested cell on a spreadsheet applied, devastatingly, to her mouth. She answered him, because her body had been counting in time with him all afternoon and the elevator was the only room in the whole tower that did not want a witness other than him. She tasted coffee gone cold and rain, and she wanted considerably more than taste.

He lifted her heel to the barre bolted along the service wall — a joke only a dancer would catch — and she laughed once, surprised out of herself, before his mouth found her throat and the laugh turned into a sound she did not recognize as hers. His jacket folded itself over the rail with more care than he was giving anything else. Hers followed. His palm found bare skin at her waist and she arched into the contact without a single count of choreography to tell her to, heat rising off her in sheets the rain on the glass could not answer.

"You put a barre in your secret elevator," she managed, breathless, accusatory, delighted. "And you let me believe the romance clause didn't exist."

"The barre is surplus equipment from the fourth-floor renovation. The disposal forms were never filed." His mouth moved along her jaw with the unbearable thoroughness of a man initialing every margin. "There is no romance clause. There is an audit, conducted after hours, of a deliverable the office has a documented interest in."

"Which deliverable?"

"Your balance," he said, and did something with his hand at the small of her back that made the answer true, made the barre necessary, made the whole municipal vocabulary between them shimmer for a second into what it had been covering all week. She gripped his lapel like a railing. The car hummed around them, patient, off the public map, keeping no minutes.

"Say stop if you mean it," he said against her collarbone.

"I mean keep auditing."

He kept auditing — thorough, merciless, counting under his breath like her body was one more thing he could bring into compliance on tempo — until her knees gave up their argument and every nerve she owned was holding its breath at the top of the phrase. And then, with the cruelest precision in the entire municipal code, he eased back.

"Deliverable three," he said, his voice gone rough at the edges. "Incomplete filing. You finish on my calendar, not on a whim in a service lift."

"Deputy Director-sama," she breathed, "that is cruel."

"That is scheduling." He pressed one more kiss to the corner of her jaw, a promise rather than a completion, and caught both her wrists and pinned them, gently, to the glass when she tried to chase the rest. "Not tonight. ## Six · Official Lentils

The next midday she cooked thank-you curry in the municipal break room and committed the grave administrative sin of bringing her personal stamp to a paper plate.

## Seven · Three Business Days

The sponsorship came back to her tablet with a fresh effective date and his signature locked at the foot of it. Not a love letter. Binding all the same, which was arguably more than a love letter could claim. She signed with a hand that still remembered the exact pressure of his fingers at her waist, and he watched the stylus and not her face until the date locked into the Council's system.

Then he stood close enough that she felt the heat of him through the wool, and the elevator's unfinished deliverable announced itself again, low in her belly.

"Three business days," he said. "Do not perform gratitude on municipal letterhead."

"Filed," she answered. "And I will be asking, in writing, for the part you denied me on glass."

His gaze dropped to her mouth and stayed there a beat too long to be procedural. "Bring that request to the couch," he said. "Not to the elevator access log. One of those I can keep off the record."

After hours, on the office couch, he loaned her a blanket against her noise. Harbor lights smeared across the window. A small pile of clementines waited on the armrest, bright as little suns he would absolutely deny having bought. She took the far cushion; he took the near one; the blanket became a shared blanket the moment she shivered and he pulled it over both of them without a word about it, the way he did everything he did not intend to put in writing.

She peeled. The citrus smell cut bright through the toner and the rain. "Still sponsorship," he agreed, and kissed her with the peel still curled in her fingers — slow and deep, the kind of kiss that made her forget municipal letterhead entirely, the kind that started as housekeeping and ended as a hearing she had no intention of surviving with dignity. She climbed into his lap under the tent of the blanket, and this time he did not stop her, did not count her down off the ledge, did not schedule her wanting for some future fiscal quarter. ## Eight · The Velvet Lantern

Three business days after the wrong conference room, Toa learned that a deputy director's chop did not only open doors. It opened field trips.

He met her at the licensing tower before dawn, which was his way of saying *the queue starts when I say it starts*. The witness clause was the price of her fast sponsorship, and she could recite it in her sleep: she would attend the inspections he ran, document what he pointed at, and stand on the public record as living proof that the deputy who had vouched for her had not invented a dancer to pad his own numbers. Favoritism insurance, written for a favoritism nobody had committed yet. *Failure to attend revokes sponsor standing.*

"Mandatory on-site witness," she read off her tablet, for the fourth time. "That sounds like liability with fluorescent lighting."

"It sounds like exactly what it is. You attend. You document. You do not perform gratitude on municipal letterhead." A beat. "Again."

"I performed gratitude once."

"You stamped a paper plate. The Council has a long memory and a worse filing system. It kept the worst half."

The wellness corridor was a string of licensed calm — massage studios, float tanks, candle rhetoric under one shared neon awning. Velvet Lantern Wellness Collective promised serenity in four competing fonts. Kaoru promised occupancy charts, fire exits, and a sponsor artist on the witness log. Inside, the lobby smelled like eucalyptus and money pretending to be mindfulness, and the staff moved the second he uncapped his pen: chairs squared to invisible lines, diffusers dialed down, a standee rotated quietly to face the wall, as if shame could be achieved at an angle.

The operating entity on the premises license made her bite the inside of her cheek: Scorpion-grey Holdings LLC.

"Cute," Kaoru said, reading it the way other people read a footnote that was about to need a lawyer. "File that under names that audition for subpoenas."

She filmed what he tilted his chin at — a peeling capacity chart, a float-tank door whose handwritten sign read *Silence is compliance*, a cluster of candles arranged to be, technically, not seating. He dictated tags under his breath, *14-C, occupancy, diffuser, exhibit*, and once read over her shoulder, close enough that she felt the wool of his suit at her arm, and told her that her spelling of *municipal* was correct. From Kitsu Kaoru, that counted as fireworks over the harbor.

"Write *diffuser exceeds serenity*," he said, deadpan, not looking up.

She typed it. "That is not a real citation."

"No. That is flirtation via footnote, To-chan, and I allow it exactly once per inspection. Now log the candle count under fire load, where it does actual work."

"You called me To-chan in a wellness closet. Is that witness work or harassment?"

"It is scheduling. You respond faster when I use the nickname inventory. I have data on it."

In the treatment-room doorway he stood with his shoulder against the frame while she filmed past him, witness posture, not stage posture. When he shifted to let a staffer squeeze by, his chest brushed her back, and heat came through tracksuit and wool like the building had a fault in the wall. His mouth came close to her ear — not a kiss, only proximity, only geometry. "Hold still," he said, the same two words as the elevator, in the same low register that did not belong on a municipal feed, and her whole body tightened around the memory like a fist. She filmed the doorway anyway. Steady frame. *Witness work*, she told her body, in his voice, because his voice was the only thing her body currently obeyed.

Her body declined the instruction and filed the moment instead under *Saturday, one bed, clerical error*.

He moved. The eucalyptus exhaled.

After, Kaoru caught her wrist. Not hard. Inventory again, cataloguing what the body said that the paperwork could not. "You held firm." His thumb moved once across the inside of her pulse, where the shaking still lived, and the touch went through her like current. "Your hands shook. I noticed. I notice things for a living."

"I noticed you noticing."

Something that was not quite the almost-smile crossed his mouth and changed its mind. "The harbor weekend will tell us whether you shake on a queen bed or only in corridors." He let go of her wrist, returning it like municipal property. "Do not read romance into that sentence. Read the rider."

Her face went hot in a way the eucalyptus could not explain. "I read the rider twice."

"Good. Read it a third time on the ferry. Riders improve with motion sickness."

## Nine · The Accompanist

He did not take her back to the tower right away. He took her to a studio.

A mirror wall, a barre, rain ticking the glass — and, against the far wall, an upright piano, municipal brown, a half-step out of tune, the lid already up as if it had been waiting all morning for someone with a reason.

"Your witness clause runs both directions today," Kaoru said, which was not a sentence she had ever expected to hear in his voice. "You have documented four of my inspections. An auditor who has never seen the deliverable cannot certify it. So I am here to witness one of yours. Run a class. I will sit where I can see you and the mirror."

So she gave him the structure, barre to reverence, translating as she went — plié, to bend; tendu, to stretch the foot along the floor; grand battement at the end, the leg thrown up and snapped down like an objection nobody bothered to sustain. He counted her under his breath, low and even, and somewhere in the rond de jambe she stopped dancing *at* the witness and simply worked, honest as the mirror, and she watched him register the difference in the glass and file it where he filed the things he had decided to keep.

For the centre she set her phone on the barre to play accompaniment, tinny through a speaker that had survived three tours. Kaoru looked at it the way he looked at a capacity chart that had already lied to him once.

"That is a recording. A recording cannot tell whether you are early or whether you are apologizing. It is a metronome that forgot it was ever supposed to be a human."

"And you could do better, Deputy Director-sama."

"I read music. It is mostly counting with better penmanship."

And then he did the thing she had spent two episodes failing to make him do across a desk. He stood, took off the charcoal jacket and folded it over the barre, rolled his sleeves to the forearm, and sat down at the municipal piano as if it were one more office the Council had assigned him. He met her on her floor, in her medium, in the one room in Ryoko Port where no signature he owned was worth a single thing.

"Adagio," she said, and her voice did something unsteady on the word. "A slow waltz, three-four, smooth, thirty-two counts. Then play it like you mean it, not like you are proving the meter is compliant."

He played it correctly. That was the first and worst thing about it — every bar squared to the edge like a citation, the left hand landing flat on the downbeat like a stamp instead of a breath. She danced it anyway, développé and promenade and the long arabesque line drawn out to where balance turned honest, and it was *fine*, and *fine* was the single most insulting word in either of their vocabularies.

She did not stop to correct him. She corrected him the way the body corrects, which is the only way that ever takes — lifted into the développé a fraction early and let her arm finish the phrase his hand had clipped short, teaching him with her spine the thing the file could not put into French: that the music was not the count. The music was what the count was *for*.

And somewhere in the second pass, it happened. The small unscheduled thing. He stopped playing the count and started playing *her*. His left hand found the breath underneath the three-four and held the dancer instead of the meter. When she suspended at the top of the développé he waited for her, the chord hanging in the rain-light until her balance asked for the next one, and he gave it to her exactly when she needed it and not a beat before. He was not a metronome anymore. He was listening. He was, against every clause he had ever drafted, *accompanying* her.

She felt it the way you feel a room change temperature. For eight bars the Deputy Director of the Office of Arts & Licensing was nobody's signatory of record. He was only the man at the piano who had decided, mid-phrase, to meet her where she actually lived, and the meeting cost him something she could see in his jaw and would never be allowed to log.

"You changed it," she said, when the phrase resolved. Her chest hurt in a way that had nothing to do with the cardio. "You stopped counting and started listening. Those are not the same skill. The first one keeps time. The second one keeps *me*."

"A recording cannot do the second one," he said, not looking up from the keys. "I find I object to being outperformed by a speaker that survived three tours. File it under professional pride and let it stay there."

"I will file it wherever lets you keep doing it."

She ran the rest of the centre to his hands, because his hands had become the only accompaniment she wanted. And when the class wound down he slowed without being asked, into something low and adagio, because he had read far enough into the file to know what came last.

Reverence. A bow to the teacher, and a bow to the accompanist, because a dancer never thanks the audience first; she thanks the people who actually held the room. There was no audience here. There was no teacher. There was only the accompanist, sleeves rolled, letting the last chord ring out under the rain.

She curtsied to the piano. To him. And he did not deny the bow. For the length of one held chord he was only the man she had thanked, and he let himself be thanked, on no letterhead at all.

"I played the first eight bars badly," he said, finally lifting his hands.

"The bad eight bars are how I know the good ones were real. Motion to strike denied. I am keeping the whole take."

He rolled his sleeves back down and reassembled the Deputy Director one button at a time, but he was slower about it than the file required, and she saw that too, and said nothing, because some witness work is kinder kept off the record.

"None of what just happened goes in the report," he said at the door.

"Then it stays off letterhead. Like everything else you actually mean. I am learning the filing system. The real things go in the drawer with no label, and you guard that drawer harder than you guard the seal."

He did not confirm it. He held the studio door for her the way a gate holds weather, and the almost-smile got as far as his eyes before he filed it — and for once, the filing took a visible second longer than it should have.

She intended to accept.

## Ten · Salt Air

Saturday on the harbor ferry schedule was a suggestion, not a sailing. Rain owned the channel the way the Arts Council owned everything that performed for money inside the city limits: quietly, in quantity, with paperwork waiting at the other end of the crossing.

Toa stood under the terminal roof in the black tracksuit with the gold crane over her heart, duffel at her feet, the rider memorized like a prayer she was not permitted to enjoy. *Not romance on letterhead.* She had written it in her notes app. She had texted it to Sango at two in the morning. She would probably dream it in his voice.

Kaoru stood three paces away, close enough to supervise and far enough to deny he was supervising, reading the ferry manifest like a man who had already filed the rain as exhibit A. Two hours late, the ferry coughed against the pier, and salt air finally beat the smell of toner out of her lungs. Somewhere between the terminal and the gangway, Toa stopped apologizing in her shoulders, and she did it without being told. Kaoru did not comment. Commenting would have been almost praise, and he rationed almost-praise more strictly than the Council rationed permits.

The harbor Airbnb was cedar shingles gone silver and a lockbox that thought it was a deputy director: stubborn, coded, certain it outranked her. Inside — one queen bed. One narrow sofa. One deputy director reading the occupancy clause aloud as if the furniture were a municipal form awaiting his chop.

"One bed," Toa said. "One deputy. That is either a rom-com or a labor violation, and I would like to know which office handles the complaint."

"It is a filing, and the office is mine. The listing logged the single bed as a clerical error. I did not correct it, because corrections require a written request, and you did not submit one." He set the wall tablet to dark. "I do not file requests on an applicant's behalf. I only sign them."

Her phone lit against her thigh. Sango, in triplicate:

*ferry delay omg*

*one bed airbnb*

*girl is this a date???*

"If you answer her *yes*," Kaoru said, watching her face and not her screen, "I am obligated to add a footnote to the file: applicant confuses calendar with courtship. If you answer *no*, answer the way the rider taught you. Dates and names only. Nothing the People and Conduct office can subpoena later."

She typed: *not a date. contract says not romance on letterhead. send snacks.*

Sango sent back a string of emojis that translated, in any language and any jurisdiction, as *sure, Jan.* And she was not wrong, which was the whole joke and the whole ache of it. The contract was the most romantic thing anyone had ever written about Toa in her life. They filed the courtship as procedure precisely so that neither of them ever had to admit it was a courtship. The rider was the flirting. The audit was the date. She had stopped being insulted by it somewhere around the service elevator, because she had started to understand it as a kind of custody instead of a kind of lie.

Kaoru slid his tablet across the kitchen counter, the Office calendar open to a Saturday that already had her name on it. "Choose how we file the weekend," he said, "before the island files us first."

Two honest boxes. Roommate mode: separate sleep surfaces, blazer on like a seatbelt, lights out at eleven. Or test the exclusive rider: after-hours access and direct deputy review with salt air instead of fluorescent light. Deliverable review. Clause stress-test. Proximity wearing a tie — or, if the audit went the way her pulse kept voting, wearing considerably less.

"You understand the rider is calendar and audit," he said, not as a question. "Not romance on letterhead."

"I understand paperwork," Toa answered, holding his eyes, letting him watch the decision arrive. "I am extremely good at paperwork when someone explains the boxes to me slowly and then watches me fill them out."

He held her gaze long enough for the rain to file three more lines on the glass. "Then choose a box."

She chose the rider. The queen bed accepted them in turn, the way a form accepts the signatures it has been holding the line open for.

## Eleven · The Audit Retreat

Rain wrote shorthand on the window. His blazer stayed folded over the chair like a man who had filed himself away for the night and meant to retrieve himself in the morning. Her tracksuit jacket followed it. He stood in shirt sleeves and charcoal trousers, the deputy director reduced to breath and hands and the particular patience he usually spent on citations, and the look he gave her then was nothing the portal had a font for.

"Deliverable one," he said, and his voice had dropped into the register he used for instructions that were not up for negotiation — the register that had been undoing her since a hearing room in the rain. "You hold still while I count."

"I can hold still," she said, already failing. "Count."

He kissed her first, mouth to mouth, the couch's unfinished filing finally paid in full, and there was nothing brief about it: slow, thorough, devastating, a man working through a document line by line and refusing to skip a single one. The zipper of her jacket gave way under his fingers with a sound like a held breath releasing. He undressed her the way he amended a filing — in order, unhurried, attentive to every margin — and every inch of skin he uncovered he treated as something he had signed for and intended to keep, until she was trembling and the rain was the only thing in the room still keeping regular time.

"Say stop if you mean it," he said against her jaw. "The audit pauses on the word."

"I mean audit," she said. "Keep going."

He kept going. He took her apart with the same merciless evenness he gave a contested spreadsheet — counting under his breath, low and even, one, two, three — and she learned that a week of *almost*, of elevators and corridors and denials filed in triplicate, had been less a cruelty than a ledger: every postponed line item now coming due at once, with interest. She heard herself beg in the only grammar he allowed after dark, dates and names only — *Saturday, harbor, Kaoru, please* — and he answered the filing the way he answered every filing he had decided to approve. Completely. On his own schedule. With his name all over it.

"Do not perform gratitude on rental linens," he murmured, somewhere in the middle of ruining her.

"I file gratitude in the break room," she gasped. "You said so. I read the rule twice."

"Good."

What the night entered into the record after that, the record kept simple: warmth, weight, the count dissolving into breath, her acceptable noise spilling into the pillow where no listing photo could see it, his composure finally — finally — failing against her neck with a sound no spreadsheet ever got to hear. They paid off every almost the week had accrued, and then, because he was a thorough man, they audited the balance twice more before the harbor went fully quiet: once with her counting out loud, because he had taught her the count and she had decided, with great satisfaction, to use it on him; once slow at the window, rain silvering the glass, his hands steady on her like a man holding something he had stopped pretending was procedure.

Between, he was exact about care the way he was exact about everything — water, the blanket, a towel warmed on the radiator, the small logistics of tenderness performed without comment, filed in advance like a man who planned even this. When she shivered in the aftermath he steadied her with a palm at her hip and reminded her, dry even now, that acceptable noise was still noise.

"Deliverable five," she whispered, daring it. "You do not perform distance on rental linens either."

"Motion denied on distance." He drew her into the curve of his body, her spine against his chest, his arm a bracket she had no intention of contesting, and let her feel exactly how little distance he had left in inventory. "Monday calendar owns the debrief. Tonight owns the rest of the docket."

Afterward she wore his shirt to the bathroom like a trophy she fully intended to deny in the morning's filing, and caught herself in the mirror — flushed, wrecked, smiling like a woman whose paperwork had finally cleared.

"File it under exclusive rider," he said from the bed.

"Already filed," she answered from the doorway. "Signed, dated, and cross-referenced."

## Twelve · Balcony Minutes

Sunday night owned the balcony. Harbor lights smeared across the rail. The municipal portal glowed on the tablet between them like a small cold moon: ferry back at six, and an HR block penciled in for Tuesday under *People and Conduct*.

"Tuesday HR," Toa said, reading the row upside down. "That sounds like the viral clip from the hearing finally grew teeth."

"It sounds like signatory pressure." He did not soften it; softening was not a service he offered. "Someone screen-recorded the Wrong Floor hearing weeks ago. Public hearings stream onto the record, so the clip is technically a public document — I cannot have it taken down, only outlived. The channel has decided that a deputy who muted a hostile gallery to seat a confused applicant must have been protecting someone. People and Conduct will have opened a file on the rumor by eight on Tuesday, before the coffee is enforceable."

"A file about us."

"A file about a conflict of interest. You are listed in the sponsorship as the dependent applicant. I am the signatory of record. HR does not investigate feelings. It investigates whether a signatory has any business signing for an applicant the whole channel now assumes he is sleeping with." A beat, dry as a stamped form. "The channel's timing is poor and its accuracy has recently improved."

His blanket from the sofa draped her shoulders, a loan against the harbor cold, the same way he loaned her warmth everywhere else: temporarily, on the record, with the quiet expectation of return. He stood close enough that her hip brushed his, and neither of them filed a complaint.

"I keep thinking about the studio," she said, because the salt air made her braver than the tower ever did. "The afternoon you put down the clipboard and played so I could take class. You stopped counting and started listening, and you have been a different kind of careful with me ever since. That is not on any letterhead. I checked the file twice."

He was quiet long enough that the harbor filed three more waves against the pilings. "The piano is not in the record," he said at last. "Neither is the reason I sat down at it. Some deliverables I keep in the drawer with no label, where the board cannot ask me to explain them." A pause, which from him was a paragraph. "You are filed in that drawer. So is the afternoon. I do not intend to move either of you out of it for anyone holding a clip."

His fingers found hers on the cold railing, laced for a moment, then separated as if the gesture had been caught on a feed. "Do not post *is this a date* with my badge in the frame. The board can survive a rumor. It cannot survive a caption."

"I will post *exclusive rider survived cross-examination* and let Sango translate it into something deniable."

"Motion denied on translation." His thumb traced her knuckle once, the most he would allow himself on an exposed balcony, and the single small touch carried more voltage than the whole night had any right to leave him. "Pack at five."

The ferry horn answered from the channel like a stamp coming down. Case two closed. Case three waited on the far side of the crossing, behind the glass doors of the HR wing and an inbox that did not care in the slightest whether the bed had been one surface or two — only whose names sat in which boxes on the packet.

---

*His side*

The Airbnb confirmation listed one bed because clerical errors were cheaper than emotional honesty and harder to subpoena. He would not have amended it unless she asked in writing. She did not ask. She chose the rider instead, out loud, eyes open, and he audited, because audit was the only verb his job title allowed for wanting.

He had denied her twice in elevators and once on a couch. The harbor was where incomplete filing came to an end, because he had run out of floors that were not already on her calendar. He learned her the way a witness should be learned — thoroughly, on the record of his own hands — and the sounds she made he filed under *acceptable noise*, and the sound he made he would deny under oath, and both of them would know the denial for what it was.

File under exclusive rider. Deny romance on letterhead. Do not deny, even privately, the way she wore his shirt back to the bed she had pretended not to want.

On the balcony he gave her Tuesday in dates, because she deserved the math: HR file, signatory pressure, dependent listing, ferry at six. He did not tell her he had already drafted the response on the ferry over, four lines, recusal left optional. He told her to pack at five instead. That was also scheduling. That was also, in the only dialect he had ever been fluent in, keeping.

## Thirteen · Eleven Seconds

Tuesday in Ryoko Port opened the way audits opened: not with a knock, with a notification. Somewhere in a server that did not care about packets, eleven seconds of footage had taught the whole channel a deputy director's voice.

*If the agenda cannot tolerate a witness, revise the agenda.*

The internet revised nothing. It played the line forty thousand times, set it to a beat, tagged the municipal account, and decided that a man who silenced a hostile councilor to protect a confused applicant was, in the grammar of the feed, *protecting his mystery girl.*

"Deputy Director-sama," Toa said, from the corner of his office, scrolling. "You are trending. Your one good sentence has a hashtag, a remix, and at least two people in the replies who think the Council is a band."

"Trending is not a docket." He watched the inbox count climb the way a man watched a tide decide whether to take the lower dock. "People & Conduct opened a file at eight this morning, before the coffee was enforceable. The Council referred it down. That is the part that matters, not the hashtag."

The top comment said *who is the dancer.* The second said *HR nightmare.* The third said the quiet part out loud — *conflict of interest much* — with a link to the Council's own public sponsorship register, because someone had already found her name in folder two and laid it next to his in folder one. The fourth was a recipe ad.

The People & Conduct wing was all glass and acoustic panels, a fishbowl built so the building could watch you stay calm while it decided things about you. The liaison slid two folders across the table with the patience of a fault line.

"Folder one," Kaoru said, because he read his own jurisdiction aloud the way other men cleared their throats. "Signatory of record, Kitsu Kaoru. Folder two, sponsorship dependent applicant, Kakita Toa. The clip put both folders in the same frame. People & Conduct would like the frame explained before the Council decides whether it likes the frame at all."

"I walked into the wrong conference room once," Toa said. "I have read the plate on every door in this building every day since. Does the file know I read the plate now? Is there a box for a woman who learned?"

The concern, the liaison said, in the level voice of someone reading policy off the inside of their own eyelids, was not the wrong door. The wrong door was an anecdote. The concern was a signatory whose name appeared beside a dependent the public had decided to find interesting. Interest was not a violation. But interest invited review, and review was what this room was for.

"Stated plainly," Kaoru translated, because translation was a thing he did for her now without being asked, "a deputy who signs for an applicant he is rumored to favor cannot be trusted to have signed cleanly, and a sponsorship signed unclean can be suspended, audited, or revoked by the people upstairs. Rumor is not a finding. But rumor schedules meetings, and we are in one, and the board that can revoke you is reading the minutes."

Back in the licensing wing, the packet lay open under the desk lamp. His chop in the signatory box. Her name in the dependent box. Two boxes, one inch of paper between them, that a camera had turned into a couple and a councilor had turned into a grievance.

He turned the monitor toward her, two ledgers side by side, the way he saw everything. The public narrative: a deputy and his favorite, a muted councilor, a mystery girl, a story with a beat you could dance to. The real calendar: thirty-one open applicants, four inspections, a ferry receipt from a harbor weekend filed under *audit retreat*, and one row left deliberately blank, where a more careful man would have written something exculpatory.

"The harbor weekend is in the calendar as audit," he said. "The internet would call it something else, and the internet would not be entirely wrong, which is the part I cannot stamp away. Both readings sit on the same row. People & Conduct will put a finger on that row and ask me to explain it, and I will have to choose, in writing, which ledger is true. They are both true. That is the trouble with ledgers."

"So the question is not whether we did anything against the rules," she said carefully, because careful was the only speed this room allowed. "We read the rules out loud. We signed the rules. The question is which version of us goes in the permanent file. The clip's version, or the calendar's."

"Correct. And the applicant gets a vote, because the applicant is the one the file outlives." He set the pen down precisely parallel to the packet, the way he set down everything he was about to make her decide. "There are two clean ways. The Council will accept either. I will sign either. But you live inside whichever one we pick."

The procedural answer sat there, clean and cold and entirely defensible. Recuse the signatory. Move her packet to Deputy Saito — a deputy with no hashtag, no harbor receipt, no almost-smile anywhere on file, a deputy who would learn her compliance and never her coffee order. A conflict resolved by subtraction. It was the right answer. It was the answer a careful applicant gave. It would also hand her, in writing, to a stranger, and erase the only honest reason she had to keep walking into this particular wrong room on purpose.

The other answer was riskier and truer and went in the same permanent file but said the opposite thing. *Signatory and applicant have a personal relationship. Signatory discloses. Applicant consents to oversight.* Not romance on letterhead — romance disclosed in the margin of the letterhead, where People & Conduct could read it and rule on it and keep it. A filing that survived the clip by agreeing with it, on their terms, on the record, indefinitely.

It did not erase the conflict. It declared it.

Toa looked at the blank row he had left on purpose, the row a more careful man would have filled with a lie, and she chose the margin.

"Then we tell the truth on a form," she said. "The whole inconvenient truth, in the boxes provided. You understand that once you write it, the port owns the sentence too. Not just the harbor. Not just the inn and the rain and the count. The city gets to keep us in a folder and read us back whenever it wants."

"I understand paperwork," he said, which was almost a joke, and which from him, on this subject, in this room, was almost a vow.

## Fourteen · The Margin

After hours, the People & Conduct fishbowl went dark across the atrium and his office kept one lamp lit, the way it always did when the building had finished watching. The couch remembered them. So did the blanket, the loan against her noise she had never officially returned. A bowl of clementines sat on the side table, the bowl someone kept restocking and denying responsibility for, and rain read the window like a slow auditor.

He had written the disclosure in his own hand. She knew, because he had shown her the line before he filed it, turned the page toward her the way he turned the monitor, so she could read it and consent to it and not be surprised by her own life later. *Personal relationship. Disclosed. Oversight accepted.* Three sentences. The port owned them now. So, apparently and on purpose, did he.

"You put us on a form and signed it," Toa said. "In your own handwriting, where it cannot be deleted by a template or a tide. That is the most romantic thing a deputy director can do, and I want it on the record that I know how low a bar that is. It is also, technically, paperwork. I am crying about paperwork. In a glass building. On municipal furniture."

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand and laughed at herself, wet and astonished. "Do you understand what you just did. Three cases of *not romance on letterhead*, three cases of filing it as procedure so the word could never be quoted against us — and then you took the word and wrote it *onto the letterhead yourself*, in ink, where a clerk can read it. You spent a year keeping us off the record to protect us. Today you put us on the record to protect us. The whole metaphor just turned over in its sleep, and I am the only witness, and I am extremely reliable."

"I stopped being able to file you as procedure somewhere around a studio piano," he said, which was the closest he had ever come, in three cases, to explaining himself. "A man cannot accompany a woman through a whole class, badly and then well, and keep pretending the clause is the true version of it. The clause was always the cover sheet. So I amended the true version instead of the cover. It was the only honest filing left on the desk, and I do not leave honest filings unsigned."

He pulled the blanket higher over her lap, the loan renewed without discussion, and his hand stayed at her hip — the steadying she had stopped pretending she did not schedule her week around. "Clementine is domestic filing. Almost-smile inventory, current status: pending, denied, pending again."

"You almost smiled when I said I cried about paperwork. I saw the requisition go in." She turned into him, knees folding under her on the cushion, gold crane catching the lamp. "I have witnesses. The witness is me. I have three cases of documented experience."

"Motion denied, To-chan." But he was already drawing the zip of her jacket down, slow enough to make the word *denied* a lie, knuckles grazing the bare line of her sternum where the crane sat over her heart, and the lamplight went honeyed and close around them. "Effective in three business days, like everything I intend to keep."

"That is too long," she said against his mouth. "I have read your processing times. You can expedite."

"Then we backdate it."

He kissed her the way he counted — thorough, unhurried, refusing her the performance of shame she kept trying to hand him — and what followed was the slowest amendment he had ever filed: line by line, layer by layer, the blanket pushed to the floor and the clementines bright and forgotten on the armrest, her name taken apart and re-signed under his breath while the rain lost track of its own minutes against the glass. She begged once, in the only language he allowed after dark — *Tuesday, office, Kaoru, please* — and he granted the motion in full, deliberate, even, load-bearing, until the harbor lights smeared long across the window and she came undone with her face buried in his neck, disclosed, declared, kept. The sound he made after, low against her hair, he would deny in the morning and file, privately, where no portal could reach it, under *kept*.

After, he steadied her with his palm bracketed at her hip and observed, again, that acceptable noise was still noise, which was as close as he came to telling her she had been loud and that he had liked it. He came back with a warm cloth and the blanket from the floor, and pulled her into the curve of his body so she could feel that the audit was not, technically, complete.

"You disclosed us," she said into his chest. "I am keeping the carbon copy."

"There is no carbon copy. There is a portal and my handwriting." His thumb traced her knuckle, slow, counting something he did not say aloud. "Both are harder to delete than a clip. That is the entire point. I chose the version of us that is difficult to erase."

The clip would loop until the channel found a louder one. The file closed quieter than it had opened: folder one and folder two now one disclosed sentence the city had agreed to carry, oversight attached and signed for. The conflict had not been hidden. It had been declared, which the remix accounts hated, because the truth was boring and you could not dance to boring. Boring was enforceable. Boring did not get revoked at the next public hearing. Boring was, it turned out, a kind of safety she had spent her whole panic-bright life refusing on principle, mistaking it for the opposite of being alive.

"Go home, To-chan," he said at the door, deputy director fully reassembled, almost-smile filed for another quarter. "Read the plate first."

She read the plate. It said his name, and his title, and the office that answered to the Council. She had been opening the right door for three cases now and pretending, every time, that she had wandered in by mistake.

She had not. Not once since the first.

## Fifteen · Effective Immediately

By the time the harbor turned cold, the clip had stopped being about her.

The feed had a new favorite: a councilor — the same board member who had once objected to her presence in a licensing hearing — had narrated nine full minutes of a budget meeting with his microphone muted, gesturing gravely at a chart nobody could hear. The internet had adopted *muted councilor energy* with the speed it reserved for men who deserved it. A budget meeting had out-performed a love story, which Toa decided was the single most municipal thing that had ever happened to her.

"Deputy Director-sama," she said, scrolling, holding the phone up so he could decline to look at it. "You have been replaced. I am no longer the mystery girl from the hearing. I have been demoted. I am, at most, a footnote with good hair."

"Footnotes outlive headlines." Kaoru did not look up from the queue. "A headline is entered for a day. A footnote stays in the file. That is the only fame worth keeping."

The People & Conduct folder had closed months ago on a single sentence in his own handwriting, and the recusal that slid her paperwork sideways to Deputy Saito had made all the rest procedural, boring, unsubpoenable. The internet, denied a scandal, had gone hunting for a man with a muted mic. The harbor had let it go.

He turned his monitor toward her then, the way he had once turned it to show her the gap between two ledgers. The calendar. The standing Friday row — the one that used to read *audit retreat* in the dry, automatic font the portal assigned to everything it did not have feelings about.

It did not read *audit retreat* anymore.

Someone had written over the row by hand. His hand. She knew his block letters the way she knew his almost-smile, which was to say better than he wanted her to. Where the portal's font had once filed a euphemism, there was ink now, and the ink said a date and a plain fact about the two of them. Not the noun of it. Not a code. The thing itself, set down on a calendar the entire licensing wing could open, that Saito could open, that People & Conduct could open and read and find nothing left to do about, because it was already disclosed.

"You filed a date on letterhead," Toa said. Her throat did the thing it did. "In your own handwriting. On the Council's own calendar. You. The man who spent three cases insisting it was not romance on letterhead."

"It was not, then." He set the pen down parallel to the edge of the desk, precise as a seal pressed onto a document. "It is, now. I amended the filing." A pause, which from him was a flourish. "The letterhead caught up to the calendar. The calendar caught up to the harbor."

"Effective when?" she asked, though she knew. She had wanted to hear him say it for three cases.

"Effective immediately. No waiting period, no review window, no three business days." He looked at her properly then, the grey eyes she had watched rule against her every single time, ruling, finally, in her favor. "I have run out of business days I am willing to make you wait."

That was when it happened. The thing he had denied for three cases. The motion he had ruled against every time she filed it, from the bench of his own face. The corner of his mouth went, and kept going, and did not stop at almost. It landed. A whole one, small and grey-eyed and entirely without precedent in any file she had ever read, and she had read all of them.

She did not breathe.

"You smiled," she whispered, like a woman reading a verdict off a page. "I saw it. I have witnesses. The witness is me. I am extremely reliable, I have done this professionally, and I am entering it into the record. It has been classified. It is load-bearing."

"Noted." He did not file it away. He did not say *motion denied*. He let it sit there on his face, where the building could have seen it if the building had cared to look. "Motion granted, To-chan. On the record."

---

They kept the harbor place now. Not as a clerical error — as a standing reservation with both their names on it and a lockbox that had finally, after a year of fumbling, learned the two of them. Rain came back the way it always came back to Ryoko Port, quietly and in quantity, and filed itself against the cedar-framed glass while she peeled a clementine from a bowl she restocked herself these days.

"You restock the clementines," she said, working a section loose. "I have done the inspection. The bowl is never empty. It refills overnight, like a municipal service. You have always restocked the clementines, and you denied it for a year."

"Domestic filing," he said, which was not a denial, which from him was a confession with the serial numbers left on.

"You still play sometimes," she said, because she had decided, this year, to start saying the things she used to only file. "The same out-of-tune municipal piano from the rehearsal studio, moved out here on a work order nobody questioned because nobody questions you. You logged it under *equipment, surplus.* I logged it under the afternoon you stopped counting and started listening, which is the afternoon you decided to keep me, whatever you wrote on the form. We have very different filing systems for the same instrument. Mine is more accurate."

He did not deny that either. He never tuned the piano. She had come to understand the half-step of wrongness in it as a thing he protected on purpose — evidence that the most important instrument in his life was the one he refused to make compliant.

The blanket on the harbor sofa was no longer a loan against her noise. It was theirs, the distinction having quietly collapsed somewhere around the week a man wrote your shared Friday into ink. She sat in witness posture out of habit — hands folded, spine honest — and he noticed, because he noticed everything. It was, technically, his job.

"You can sit however you want," he said. "You are not a witness here. There is no hearing. You are not being deposed. You are the filing."

"I like the posture." She uncurled anyway, slow, joint by joint, into something with no apology left in it at all. "It is how I sit when I am paying attention. And I am paying attention, Deputy Director-sama. Close attention. Compliance-grade."

He took the clementine peel out of her fingers and set it on the armrest, bright and forgotten, the way it had been forgotten on a narrower couch in a glass building once. Then he drew the zip of her tracksuit down, slow, the way he did everything he intended to keep, knuckles tracing the bare line of her sternum where the gold crane sat warm over her heart, and the rain kept its own minutes against the glass and entered nothing into evidence.

"Acceptable noise is still noise," he murmured, the way he had once explained a clause to her, patient and exact. "The harbor has ears. The neighbors have a lease."

"Then audit it quietly." She arched up into his hand, deliberate, learning him back. "You are the one who taught me the difference."

He read her that night like a filing he had reviewed a hundred times and still wanted to read again — in order, without skipping a line, the old counting gone soft at the edges, the cadence of a man witnessing rather than a man rushing. She answered him in dates and names and nothing she would be ashamed to read back — *Friday, harbor, Kaoru, please* — and he granted every motion she entered, slow and thorough and entirely on the record of their own skin, the standing Friday made flesh and kept, until the harbor lights smeared long across the wet glass and neither of them had a single denial left in inventory. The sound he used to swear, under three cases, that he would deny — he did not deny it now, and would not deny it again.

After, he steadied her with his palm bracketed at her hip, the same hold from a service elevator and a western inn and a couch the city used to think it owned, and pulled her into the curve of his body, close, so she could feel along the whole length of him that the audit was, technically, never complete, and was never meant to be.

"Three business days," she said into his chest, testing the old phrase the way you press a healed place to be sure it is healed. "Used to be how long I had to wait for everything you intended to keep. The blanket. The verdict. You."

"You do not wait anymore." His thumb traced the line of her knuckle, back and forth, his version of saying a thing twice. "That clause is closed. Struck. You are not a deliverable with an effective date, To-chan. You are not pending. You are a standing item."

---

On the calendar back at the tower, he had left exactly one row blank. On purpose. He had told her once that a man should keep one row for the thing he had not scheduled yet: the next case, the next door, the next *unless*. He had stopped leaving the rest of them blank around the time he stopped pretending the harbor was incidental. But he kept the one. That was the most he had ever disclosed to anyone, in handwriting, on a calendar where the building could read it and the harbor, for once, could not.

Toa peeled a second clementine she had not asked permission to take, because some habits were load-bearing and this was one of them. The rain wrote shorthand on the glass, fast and unfiled. She had been reading the plate beside the door for a long time now — *Office of Arts & Licensing, Ryoko Port*, his name set under the Director's — and she no longer pretended, to him or to herself, that she had ever wandered in by mistake.

She had not. Not once since the first time.

She filed that under *kept*, in the one place no portal could index and no folder could close, and let the harbor keep its own minutes against the glass.

---

*His side*

He had ruled against the almost-smile for three cases because a smile was a finding, and findings could be entered into evidence against a man who kept his evenings off every ledger he was sworn to keep. Then, one folder at a time, he had run out of ledgers worth protecting it from. The disclosure went into the permanent file in his own hand. The internet found a louder fool with a muted mic. The harbor stopped being a thing the Council could subpoena and became, simply, Friday.

The instruments were all still his — the signatory line, the licenses, the inspections, the witness clause. There was one more instrument now, an upright with a half-step lie in it, that he had never once ruled on, because it was the only one in his custody that had ever ruled on *him*. He kept it out of tune on purpose. A man should keep one instrument the building cannot make compliant.

*Not romance on letterhead* had been true once, true the way most of his denials were true, right up until she made keeping them more expensive than letting them go. So he amended the filing. He struck the euphemism. He let the smile land, in front of her, where it counted as testimony. He told her there was no waiting period, which was, he was fairly sure, the truest thing his job title had ever permitted him to say out loud.

He left one row blank, for the next *unless*. He filled in all the rest, in ink, where the wing could read them. And she told him she had memorized his name a year ago, in a conference room she had walked into entirely on purpose, and had only ever pretended otherwise to make him work for it.

That was not a finding. It was a fact. Disclosed, oversight accepted, effective immediately.
