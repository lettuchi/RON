# Effective Immediately (Modern AU Epilogue)

**Bonus prose · not canon.** Third person, mostly Toa POV. Coda to the Modern AU trilogy and tone reference for a future playable epilogue scene. Sequel to [Permits & HR](./ryoko-owari-au-modern-case3-permits-hr.md).

---

By the time the harbor turned cold, the clip had stopped being about her.

It had taken months. Months since the People & Conduct folder, the one HR kept calling a matter and she kept calling a fishbowl, had closed on a single sentence she had chosen herself. Months since the building stopped angling its glass to catch her in the background of someone else's footage. Long enough that the cold came back to Ryoko Port the way it always did, off the water and in through the seams, and found her already installed, like a fixture nobody had filed a removal order on.

Toa found out the way she found out everything in Ryoko Port now: on her phone, in the corner of an office that had learned her coffee order before it learned her last name. The feed had a new favorite. The Arts Council, the municipal board that funded the sponsorships and licensed the storefronts and convened its hearings live onto the public record, had held a budget meeting, and one of its councilors (the same board member who had objected to her presence in the licensing hearing she now thought of, privately, as the first one) had narrated nine full minutes of it with his microphone muted. He had gestured at a chart nobody could hear. He had pointed, gravely, at a number, and mouthed what was clearly a very important word, and the stream had carried all of it in perfect silence onto the record the Council was so proud of keeping. The internet had adopted *muted councilor energy* with the speed it reserved for men who deserved it.

The clip that used to be hers, the one from the licensing hearing where a deputy director slid a folder six inches across a table toward a sponsored artist and the camera caught her not breathing, had finally fallen off the front of the feed. Replaced. A budget meeting had out-performed a love story, which she decided was the single most municipal thing that had ever happened to her.

"Deputy Director-sama," she said, scrolling, holding the phone up so he could decline to look at it. "You have been replaced. The channel found a louder clip. I am no longer the mystery girl from the hearing. I have been demoted. I am, at most, a footnote with good hair."

"Footnotes outlive headlines." Kaoru did not look up from the queue, where (she knew without seeing it) a column of venue applications and storefront license renewals waited for the signature only he could give them. "A headline is entered for a day. A footnote stays in the file. That is the only fame worth keeping."

Across the desk he was still in the charcoal suit he wore like a sealed envelope, jacket on, tie exact, even now, even here in the after-hours quiet where there was no one left to be precise for but her. The crane on her tracksuit caught the lamp instead, gold over her heart, gold stripes down the arms, the same black jacket she had worn into the wrong conference room a year of queue ago. She owned newer ones now; he had, at some point and without comment, started buying them, which was its own kind of disclosure. She kept wearing this one. It had stopped apologizing in the shoulders somewhere around the harbor, and she did not trust the newer ones to remember how to do that yet.

The fishbowl had gone quiet months back, but she still thought of it in the present tense, the way you keep flinching at a sound after it stops. People & Conduct had opened a folder with two names in it: Kitsu Kaoru, Deputy Director, Office of Arts & Licensing, signatory of record; and one Council-sponsored artist who had let him be exactly that. The building had wanted a story it could stamp. A finding. A sanction. A clause attached to a sponsorship, something it could refer upward to the board and downward into the portal. It had never gotten one. The folder had closed instead on a single sentence in his own handwriting, the kind he refused to trust the portal's font with: *Personal relationship. Disclosed. Oversight accepted.* He had signed it, dated it, and walked it to the permanent file himself, and the recusal that slid her paperwork sideways to Deputy Saito had made all the rest procedural, boring, unsubpoenable. The internet, denied a scandal, had gone hunting for a man with a muted mic. The harbor had let it go.

He turned his monitor toward her then, the way he had once turned it across a conference table to show her the gap between two ledgers. The calendar, this time. The whole Office of Arts & Licensing ran on it, the entire licensing wing keyed to one shared grid: thirty-one applicants in intake, four compliance inspections booked against four storefronts, a public hearing flagged in the Council's color. And the standing Friday row. The one that used to read *audit retreat* in the dry, automatic font the portal assigned to everything it did not have feelings about.

It did not read *audit retreat* anymore.

Someone had written over the row by hand. His hand. She knew his block letters the way she knew his almost-smile, which was to say better than he wanted her to. Where the portal's font had once filed a euphemism, there was ink now, and the ink said a date and a plain fact about the two of them. Not the noun of it. Not a code. The thing itself, set down on a calendar the entire licensing wing could open, that Saito could open, that People & Conduct could open and read and find nothing left to do about, because it was already disclosed.

"You filed a date on letterhead," Toa said. Her throat did the thing it did, the thing she would have called witness-stand nerves if she were under oath, which, around him, she always sort of felt she was. "In your own handwriting. On the Council's own calendar. You. The man who spent three cases insisting it was not romance on letterhead."

"It was not, then." He set the pen down parallel to the edge of the desk, precise as a seal pressed onto a document. "It is, now. I amended the filing." A pause, which from him was a flourish. "The letterhead caught up to the calendar. The calendar caught up to the harbor."

"You spent three cases teaching me what *on letterhead* meant," she said. "That filing us as real was the one motion you would not grant, from the bench of your own face, every single time I tried to enter it. And now you have granted it in your own hand, on the Council's own grid, where the building keeps the things it cannot take back. That is the part I did not expect. You did not keep us off the record because you were ashamed of us. You kept us off it so the port could not hold us over either of us. And the day you decided the port no longer could, you put us *on* it. The whole metaphor ran backward and landed on its feet."

"Effective when?" she asked, though she knew. She wanted to hear him say it. She had wanted to hear him say it for three cases.

"Effective immediately. No waiting period, no review window, no three business days." He looked at her properly then, the grey eyes she had watched rule against her every single time, ruling, finally, in her favor. "I have run out of business days I am willing to make you wait."

That was when it happened. The thing he had denied for three cases. The motion he had ruled against every time she filed it, from the bench of his own face. The corner of his mouth went, and kept going, and did not stop at almost. It landed. A whole one, small and grey-eyed and entirely without precedent in any file she had ever read, and she had read all of them.

She did not breathe.

"You smiled," she whispered, like a woman reading a verdict off a page. "I saw it. I have witnesses. The witness is me. I am extremely reliable, I have done this professionally, and I am entering it into the record. It has been classified. It is load-bearing."

"Noted." He did not file it away. He did not square it back into the neutral line he had held across three cases. He did not say *motion denied*. He let it sit there on his face, where the building could have seen it if the building had cared to look, where the muted councilor and the whole board and all of People & Conduct could have stamped it and could do precisely nothing. "Motion granted, To-chan. On the record."

---

They kept the harbor place now. Not as a clerical error, the way it had started, a booking he could plausibly tell a folder was incidental. As a standing reservation with both their names on it and a lockbox that had finally, after a year of fumbling, learned the two of them. Rain came back the way it always came back to Ryoko Port, quietly and in quantity, off the water and onto everything, and filed itself against the cedar-framed glass in long patient columns while she peeled a clementine from a bowl she restocked herself these days.

"You restock the clementines," she said, working a section loose. "I have done the inspection. The bowl is never empty. It refills overnight, like a municipal service. You have always restocked the clementines, and you denied it for a year."

"Domestic filing," he said, which was not a denial, which from him was a confession with the serial numbers left on.

"You still play sometimes," she said, because she had decided, this year, to start saying the things she used to only file. "When you think the building has stopped listening. The same out-of-tune municipal piano from the rehearsal studio, moved out to the harbor place on a work order nobody questioned because nobody questions you. You logged it under *equipment, surplus.* I logged it under the afternoon you stopped counting and started listening, which is the afternoon you decided to keep me, whatever you wrote on the form. We have very different filing systems for the same instrument. Mine is more accurate."

He did not deny that either. He never tuned the piano. She had come to understand the half-step of wrongness in it as a thing he protected on purpose, the way he protected the one blank calendar row, evidence that the most important instrument in his life was the one he refused to make compliant.

The blanket on the harbor sofa was no longer a loan against her noise, the thing he had once handed her with the face of a man releasing evidence into temporary custody. It was hers now, or theirs, the distinction having quietly collapsed somewhere around the week a man wrote your shared Friday into ink on a calendar the licensing wing could read. She sat in witness posture out of habit: hands folded, spine honest, the way you sit when someone might ask you, under oath, to account for where you had been. He noticed, because he noticed everything. It was, technically, his job.

"You can sit however you want," he said. "You are not a witness here. There is no hearing. You are not being deposed. You are the filing."

"I like the posture." She uncurled anyway, slow, joint by joint, into something with no apology left in it at all. "It is how I sit when I am paying attention. And I am paying attention, Deputy Director-sama. Close attention. Compliance-grade."

He took the clementine peel out of her fingers and set it on the armrest, bright and forgotten, the way it had been forgotten on a narrower couch in a glass building once, the night neither of them had filed correctly. Then he drew the zip of her tracksuit down, slow, the way he did everything he intended to keep, knuckles tracing the bare line of her sternum where the gold crane sat warm over her heart.

"Acceptable noise is still noise," he murmured, the way he had once explained a clause to her, patient and exact. "The harbor has ears. The neighbors have a lease. Both are on the record."

"Then audit it quietly." She arched up into his hand, deliberate, learning him back. "You are the one who taught me the difference. Acceptable. Documented. Off the record where it counts."

He undressed her like a man amending a filing he had read a hundred times and still wanted to read again, in order, without skipping a line: the jacket open first, then the sports bra, then the leggings down her dancer's legs while the rain kept its own minutes against the glass and entered nothing into evidence. When his mouth found her she begged the only way he had ever allowed after dark, in dates and names and nothing she would be ashamed to read back, *Friday, harbor, Kaoru, please*, and he counted under his breath, the old counting, one, two, three, unhurried, the cadence of a man witnessing rather than a man rushing, refusing her the performance of shame and refusing, just as steadily, to stop, until her heel pressed a careful bruise into his shoulder and the rain lost track of itself on the glass.

"You do not perform gratitude on the furniture," he said against her hip, and it was almost the old line, the one he had used to hold a line between them, except his voice had a smile folded into it now, the one he was no longer denying he owned.

"I file gratitude in the break room," she gasped. "You said so. Repeatedly. On more than one occasion. It is precedent now, and you taught me to respect precedent."

He rose over her, hard and bare in the lamp light, and she watched him the way she had finally stopped pretending not to, openly, on the record of her own face. Then he covered them both in the same patient order he brought to everything, and slid into her in one slow stroke that bowed her up off the cushion, full and choosing it, all of her, with her whole disclosed self and no clause held back. He counted, low, and she met him on the count, hips answering his, the gold crane catching light in the narrow space between their bodies, the standing Friday made flesh and kept. When he told her to come, on the word, the way he gave every instruction that mattered, she did, clenching around him with her face buried in his neck and the harbor lights smeared long across the wet glass, and he followed her down deep with a sound he used to swear, under three cases, that he would deny, and did not deny now, and would not deny again.

After, he steadied her with his palm bracketed at her hip, the same hold from a service elevator and a western inn and a couch the city used to think it owned. He dealt with the practical filing he still carried out like a man who planned even this, then came back with the blanket from the floor and pulled her into the curve of his body, close, so she could feel along the whole length of him that the audit was, technically, never complete, and was never meant to be.

"Three business days," she said into his chest, testing the old phrase the way you press a healed place to be sure it is healed. "Used to be how long I had to wait for everything you intended to keep. The blanket. The verdict. You."

"You do not wait anymore." His thumb traced the line of her knuckle, back and forth, a small repeated motion, his version of saying a thing twice. "That clause is closed. Struck. You are not a deliverable with an effective date, To-chan. You are not pending. You are a standing item."

---

On the calendar back at the tower, on the shared grid the whole Office of Arts & Licensing ran on, he had left exactly one row blank. On purpose. The way he used to leave one blank, back when leaving it blank was the only freedom his title allowed him. She had asked him about it once, early, when she still thought every empty field was an oversight waiting to be corrected. He had told her a man should keep one row for the thing he had not scheduled yet: the next case, the next door, the next *unless*. The next time the work handed him something he had not seen coming and could not have filed for in advance. He had stopped leaving the rest of them blank around the time he stopped pretending the harbor was incidental. But he kept the one. That was the most he had ever disclosed to anyone, in handwriting, on a calendar where the building could read it and the harbor, for once, could not.

Toa peeled a second clementine she had not asked permission to take, because some habits were load-bearing and this was one of them. The rain wrote shorthand on the glass, fast and unfiled. She had been reading the plate beside the door before she let herself through it for a long time now, *Office of Arts & Licensing, Ryoko Port*, his name set under the Director's, Saito's beside his, and she no longer pretended, to him or to herself, that she had ever wandered in by mistake.

She had not. Not once since the first time.

She filed that under *kept*, in the one place no portal could index and no folder could close, and let the harbor keep its own minutes against the glass.

---

*His side*

He had ruled against the almost-smile for three cases because a smile was a finding, and findings could be entered into evidence against a man who kept his evenings off every ledger he was sworn to keep. A deputy director did not get to have an unguarded face. Not in a hearing, not in the fishbowl, not anywhere a sponsorship he had signed could be reopened on the strength of it. So he had ruled against it, every time, from the bench of his own mouth. Then, one folder at a time, he had run out of ledgers worth protecting it from. The People & Conduct matter closed. The disclosure went into the permanent file in his own hand. The internet found a louder fool with a muted mic. The harbor stopped being a thing the Council could subpoena and became, simply, Friday.

The instruments were all still his. The signatory line, the venue and storefront licenses, the compliance inspections, the witness clause he had once administered over a sponsorship that happened to carry her name. He had held every one of them across a desk from her and ruled correctly, every time, which was its own slow form of wanting. There was one more instrument now, an upright with a half-step lie in it, that he had never once held across a desk and never once ruled on, because it was the only one in his custody that had ever ruled on *him*. He had played it correctly before he had played it well; he had told her so, in a studio, as the only review he would ever file on himself. He kept it out of tune on purpose. A man should keep one instrument the building cannot make compliant. He wrote the date on letterhead in his own hand because he did not trust the portal's font with the sentence, the same reason he had written the disclosure himself rather than let an intake form phrase it for him. *Not romance on letterhead* had been true once, true the way most of his denials were true, right up until she made keeping them more expensive than letting them go. So he amended the filing. He struck the euphemism. He let the smile land, in front of her, where it counted as testimony. He told her there was no waiting period, which was, he was fairly sure, the truest thing his job title had ever permitted him to say out loud.

He left one row blank, for the next *unless*. He filled in all the rest, in ink, where the wing could read them. He told her to read the plate, out of habit, the way he told her everything that mattered twice, and she told him she had memorized his name a year ago, in a conference room she had walked into entirely on purpose, and had only ever pretended otherwise to make him work for it.

That was not a finding. It was a fact. Disclosed, oversight accepted, effective immediately.

### Scene beats a playable epilogue could dramatize

- **Cold open, dethroned.** Toa shows Kaoru he has been replaced in the feed by *muted councilor energy*; she is no longer the mystery girl, the HR folder is long closed. Re-establishes world, time skip, and the internet moving on. Bureau-comedy banter.
- **The letterhead inversion.** Kaoru turns the monitor: the standing Friday row, once *audit retreat*, is now a date written in his own handwriting where HR can read it. Optional player beat: accept "effective immediately" or (joke) ask for the old three-business-day waiting period back.
- **The almost-smile lands.** The motion he denied for three cases finally carries. "Motion granted, To-chan. On the record." Strong CG candidate (a real, un-denied smile).
- **Harbor domestic / witness-posture callback.** The kept Airbnb, clementines she now restocks, the blanket that stopped being a loan. Light choice: sit in witness posture vs. sit however she wants (low-stakes intimacy beat).
- **Optional R18 audit branch.** One tasteful-explicit beat at the Case 2 / Case 3 ceiling (his control and counting, her POV desire), gated behind a consent-flavored choice and fade-capable for the build.
- **Closure stinger.** The single blank calendar row kept "for the next unless," rain on glass, "you are a standing item." Final card mirrors Case 3's blank-row / rain-on-glass imagery as a deliberate series ending.
