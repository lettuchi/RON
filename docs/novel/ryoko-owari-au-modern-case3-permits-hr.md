# Permits & HR (Modern AU Case 3)

**Bonus prose · not canon.** Third person, mostly Toa POV. Play the VN episode: `game/au_modern_case3_permits_hr.rpy`. Prequel: [Island Weekend](./ryoko-owari-au-modern-case2-island-weekend.md). Caps the Modern AU trilogy.

---

Tuesday in Ryoko Port opened the way audits opened: not with a knock, with a notification. Rain filed itself against the licensing tower in even columns, the way it filed itself against everything the Arts Council owned, and somewhere in a server that did not care about packets, eleven seconds of footage had taught the whole channel a deputy director's voice.

*If the agenda cannot tolerate a witness, revise the agenda.*

Someone had screen-recorded the Wrong Floor hearing weeks ago, back when Kakita Toa was only an expired transfer letter in the wrong chair. It had been a routine Council session, the kind the Ryoko Port Arts Council convened in public and streamed onto the record: venues to license, storefronts to permit, sponsorships to fund or sanction, all of it read aloud for anyone who cared to watch the city decide who got to make art in it. A councilor on the board had objected to the confused applicant in the gallery. The deputy director running the floor had muted him with two keystrokes and one sentence, and the record had kept the sentence forever, the way records did.

The internet revised nothing. It simply played the line forty thousand times, set it to a beat, tagged the municipal account, and decided that a man who silenced a hostile councilor to protect a confused applicant was, in the grammar of the feed, *protecting his mystery girl.*

Toa watched the clip loop on her phone in the corner of Kitsu Kaoru's office, gold crane on black at her chest, thumb scrolling a caste system of strangers who had strong opinions about a woman they had cropped out of frame.

"Deputy Director-sama," she said. "You are trending. Your one good sentence has a hashtag, a remix, and at least two people in the replies who think the Council is a band."

"Trending is not a docket." He did not look at the clip. He watched the inbox count climb the way a man watched a tide decide whether to take the lower dock. "Sit where I can see both you and the inbox. People & Conduct opened a file at eight this morning, before the coffee was enforceable. The Council referred it down to them. That is the part that matters, not the hashtag."

People & Conduct was the municipal arm that handled the parts of governance that could not be stamped into compliance. It did not investigate feelings; it had no jurisdiction over feelings and said so on its own letterhead. It investigated conflicts of interest, and it had exactly one question about the two of them: whether a deputy director who signed an artist's sponsorship had any business signing for an artist the whole channel now assumed he favored.

Behind that question stood the Council, and behind the Council stood teeth. The Ryoko Port Arts Council funded sponsorships and it could un-fund them. It could suspend Toa's sponsorship while the file stayed open. It could revoke it outright if the file went badly. It could order an audit of every permit her signatory had ever touched, attach new riders and oversight clauses to her name, or simply reassign the signatory and let a cleaner deputy carry her packet. People & Conduct was only the room where the Council decided which of those it wanted. The remedy would be paperwork either way, reassignment or disclosure or a worse word still, and it would go in a permanent file that outlived the clip, the hashtag, and probably the deputy. (The harbor weekend on his calendar, logged as audit retreat, did not help. One ferry receipt, one weekend, one row a hostile reader could translate. He had read this Tuesday block off a balcony tablet two nights ago and known it was coming, the way he knew most things were coming, in advance and in writing.)

The top comment said *who is the dancer.* The second said *HR nightmare.* The third said the quiet part out loud, *conflict of interest much,* with a link to the Council's own public sponsorship register, because someone had already found her name in folder two and laid it next to his in folder one. The fourth was a recipe ad. Someone had clipped the exact frame where he silenced the councilor with two keystrokes and captioned it *deputy protects mystery girl.* Toa read it twice. She had a name. She had a current packet, a sponsorship that paid her rent and routed through one man's signature. She had, apparently, also become a frame.

---

The People & Conduct wing was all glass and acoustic panels, a fishbowl built so the building could watch you stay calm while it decided things about you. The liaison was named in the calendar only as *People & Conduct,* no first name, no face the feed could find, and they slid two folders across the table with the patience of a fault line that had all the time in the world.

"Folder one," Kaoru said, because he read his own jurisdiction aloud the way other men cleared their throats. "Signatory of record, Kitsu Kaoru, Deputy Director, Office of Arts & Licensing. The office that answers to the Council, which is the body that pays for the sponsorship in folder two." He set the second folder square to the table edge. "Folder two, sponsorship dependent applicant, Kakita Toa. The clip put both folders in the same frame. People & Conduct would like the frame explained before the Council decides whether it likes the frame at all."

"I walked into the wrong conference room once," Toa said. "The Wrong Floor. I have read the plate on every door in this building every day since. Does the file know I read the plate now? Is there a box for a woman who learned?"

The liaison said, in the level voice of someone reading policy off the inside of their own eyelids, that the concern was not the wrong door. The wrong door was an anecdote. The concern was a signatory of record whose name appeared, in the Council's own files, beside a dependent the public had decided to find interesting. Interest was not a violation. But interest invited review, and review was what this room was for.

"The concern is conflict of interest," Kaoru translated, because translation was a thing he did for her now without being asked. "Stated plainly: a deputy who signs for an applicant he is rumored to favor cannot be trusted to have signed cleanly, and a sponsorship signed unclean can be suspended, audited, or revoked by the people upstairs. Rumor is not a finding. But rumor schedules meetings, and we are in one, and the board that can revoke you is reading the minutes."

Toa folded her hands on her knee in witness posture, not stage posture. She had learned the difference in this building, in this exact kind of room, the hard way and then the harder way. "So the rider says *not romance on letterhead,*" she said. "And we agreed with the letterhead. We have always agreed with the letterhead. The internet did not read the letterhead. The internet read a frame and wrote fan fiction, and now the fan fiction is in a municipal folder with my rent stapled to it."

"Before the liaison files anything," Kaoru said, "we decide the public posture. Not the legal posture; that comes later, on a form, in my handwriting. The public one. The clip is loud, To-chan. Our answer should be quieter. But quiet is not the same as absent, and absent reads as guilty. So choose. Even silence is a posture if you pick it on purpose."

She thought about owning it. Three sentences under her own handle, plain as a queue number, the kind of statement a person posted once and then declined to explain ever again: *The deputy seated a confused applicant and corrected a packet error. There is no story. There is a queue, and I was standing in it.* A microphone was a real strategy. Sango could draft it in ten minutes and it would trend by lunch. A microphone was also a microphone, and microphones had a way of inviting follow-up questions she did not have clean answers to, like why the audit retreat had been booked with one bed.

She thought about silence. Witness posture all the way down. Sit in the comments for a week and answer none of them and let the channel exhaust itself on a sentence that was already weeks old. Harder to misquote a woman who said nothing. Slower to clear her name, if a name could be cleared by waiting at all. Silence let the frame stand, and a standing frame was its own kind of caption.

In the end she did what the building had spent three cases teaching her to do. She answered in the register that could be enforced and let the quiet parts stay quiet, dates and names and nothing the Council could not verify against its own record. Whichever she chose, Kaoru only nodded once and said, "Dates and names only, even in a caption. Especially in a caption," and she understood that he was not managing her. He was managing the gap between two ledgers, the one the city read aloud and the one he kept in his own hand, and she had just become a line in both.

---

Back in the licensing wing of Arts & Licensing the sponsorship packet lay open under the desk lamp, the actual document, not a screenshot of it. His chop in the signatory box. Her name in the dependent box. Two boxes, one inch of paper between them, that a camera had turned into a couple and a councilor had turned into a grievance.

"Signatory of record means I answer for your compliance," Kaoru said. "When you file clean, that is my name vouching for it. When you file late, that is my name explaining why. Sponsorship dependent means your right to work in this port routes through my signature, and the Council that lets me hold the pen can decide, on a Tuesday, to suspend the sponsorship, audit every line of it, or revoke it outright. That is leverage. Mine over your livelihood, theirs over mine, all of it stacked on one chop. I do not enjoy being someone's leverage. Including, for the record, my own."

"You sound like a man reading his own name as a liability." She tilted her head, studying him the way she studied a mirror before a correction. "I have practiced that face in studio mirrors. The shoulders go in, the chin drops. It is, technically, terrible port de bras, and you wear it like it is load-bearing."

"It is load-bearing," he said. "That is the problem with the face."

He almost answered the rest of it. She watched the almost happen, watched the corner of his mouth consider the joke and decline it, and watched him file it where he filed everything he intended to keep, somewhere between *almost-smile denied* and the blank row on his calendar she was not supposed to have seen.

He turned the monitor toward her instead of finishing the sentence. Two ledgers, side by side, the way he saw everything.

The public narrative, assembled by the feed: a deputy director and his favorite. A muted councilor. A mystery girl. A story with a beat you could dance to.

The deputy's real calendar, assembled by the Council's scheduling system: thirty-one open applicants, four compliance inspections this month, a venue license appeal on Thursday, a storefront permit he intended to deny on its merits, a ferry receipt from a harbor weekend filed under *audit retreat,* and one row left deliberately blank, where a more careful man would have written something exculpatory.

"The harbor weekend is in the calendar as audit," he said. "The internet would call it something else, and the internet would not be entirely wrong, which is the part I cannot stamp away. Both readings sit on the same row. People & Conduct will put a finger on that row and ask me to explain it, and I will have to choose, in writing, which ledger is true. They are both true. That is the trouble with ledgers."

Her face went warm. The harbor weekend was a western inn and rain on glass and his voice counting low and even, and she had filed her own gratitude in places no portal could index. "So the question is not whether we did anything against the rules," she said carefully, because careful was the only speed this room allowed. "We read the rules out loud. We signed the rules. The question is which version of us goes in the permanent file. The clip's version, or the calendar's."

"Correct. And the applicant gets a vote, because the applicant is the one the file outlives. I will be a former deputy someday. You will be a woman with a sponsorship history." He set the pen down precisely parallel to the packet, the way he set down everything he was about to make her decide. "So choose how we resolve the signatory conflict before the liaison chooses a template for us. There are two clean ways. The Council will accept either. I will sign either. But you live inside whichever one we pick."

The procedural answer sat there, clean and cold and entirely defensible. Recuse the signatory. Move her packet to Deputy Saito, a deputy with no hashtag, no harbor receipt, no almost-smile anywhere on file, a deputy who would learn her compliance and never her coffee order. A different chop in the signatory box. A conflict resolved by subtraction. He would stop being her leverage. She would stop being his liability. The clip would have nothing left to point at, because the thing it pointed at would no longer be true.

It was the right answer. It was the answer a careful applicant gave. It would also hand her, in writing, to a stranger, and erase the only honest reason she had to keep walking into this particular wrong room on purpose.

The other answer was riskier and truer and went in the same permanent file but said the opposite thing. *Signatory and applicant have a personal relationship. Signatory discloses. Applicant consents to oversight.* The signatory does not recuse; the signatory tells the Council exactly what the clip suspects, in advance, in his own words, and accepts whatever oversight the board wants to attach to the sponsorship after. Not romance on letterhead. Romance disclosed in the margin of the letterhead, where People & Conduct could read it and rule on it and keep it. A filing that survived the clip by agreeing with it, on their terms, on the record, indefinitely.

It did not erase the conflict. It declared it, which the careful answer would never do.

Toa looked at the blank row he had left on purpose, the row a more careful man would have filled with a lie, and she chose the margin.

"Then we tell the truth on a form," she said. "The whole inconvenient truth, in the boxes provided. You understand that once you write it, the port owns the sentence too. Not just the harbor. Not just the inn and the rain and the count. The city gets to keep us in a folder and read us back whenever it wants."

"I understand paperwork," he said, which was almost a joke, and which from him, on this subject, in this room, was almost a vow.

---

After hours the People & Conduct fishbowl went dark across the atrium and his office kept one lamp lit, the way it always did when the building had finished watching. The couch remembered them. So did the blanket, the loan against her noise she had never officially returned. A bowl of clementines sat on the side table, the bowl someone kept restocking and denying responsibility for, and rain read the window like a slow auditor who had not yet found anything but intended to keep looking.

He had written the disclosure in his own hand, she knew, because he had shown her the line before he filed it, turned the page toward her the way he turned the monitor, so she could read it and consent to it and not be surprised by her own life later. *Personal relationship. Disclosed. Oversight accepted.* Three sentences. The port owned them now. So, apparently and on purpose, did he.

"You put us on a form and signed it," Toa said. "In your own handwriting, where it cannot be deleted by a template or a tide. That is the most romantic thing a deputy director can do, and I want it on the record that I know how low a bar that is. It is also, technically, paperwork. I am crying about paperwork. In a glass building. On municipal furniture."

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand and laughed at herself, wet and astonished. "On letterhead. Do you understand what you just did. Three cases of *not romance on letterhead*, three cases of filing it as procedure so the word could never be quoted against us, and then you took the word and wrote it *onto the letterhead yourself*, in ink, where a clerk can read it. You spent a year keeping us off the record to protect us. Today you put us on the record to protect us. The whole metaphor just turned over in its sleep, and I am the only witness, and I am extremely reliable."

"I stopped being able to file you as procedure somewhere around a studio piano," he said, which was the closest he had ever come, in three cases, to explaining himself. "A man cannot accompany a woman through a whole class, badly and then well, and keep pretending the clause is the true version of it. The clause was always the cover sheet. So I amended the true version instead of the cover. It was the only honest filing left on the desk, and I do not leave honest filings unsigned."

"The quiet parts are not on any portal." He pulled the blanket higher over her lap, the loan renewed without discussion, and his hand stayed at her hip the way it had stayed in a service elevator and a harbor inn, a steadying she had stopped pretending she did not schedule her week around. "Clementine is domestic filing. Almost-smile inventory, current status: pending, denied, pending again."

"You almost smiled when I said I cried about paperwork. I saw the requisition go in." She turned into him, knees folding under her on the cushion, gold crane catching the lamp. "I have witnesses. The witness is me. I am extremely reliable. I have three cases of documented experience."

"Motion denied, To-chan." But he was already drawing the zip of her tracksuit jacket down, slow enough to make the word *denied* a lie, knuckles grazing the bare line of her sternum where the gold crane sat over her heart. "Effective in three business days, like everything I intend to keep."

"That is too long," she said against his mouth. "I have read your processing times. You can expedite."

"Then we backdate it." He kissed her the way he counted, thorough, unhurried, refusing her the performance of shame she kept trying to hand him. His tie went over the arm of the couch, charcoal on charcoal. Her jacket followed, then the sports bra, and his palm closed warm over her breast until she arched and made a sound that did not belong in a glass building owned by a municipal council.

"Acceptable noise is still noise," he murmured, and lowered her back into the cushions with the care of a man setting down something he had signed for.

"Then audit it." She was bold the way she was bold at the barre, clean lines, no apology, the one place where performance and honesty had always been the same thing. "Quietly. The building still has ears, and at least one of them reports to HR."

He undressed her the rest of the way with the patience of a man amending a filing line by line, leggings down her dancer's legs, blanket pushed to the floor, clementines bright and forgotten on the armrest. When he knelt and put his mouth on her she bit the heel of her hand and begged in the only language he allowed after dark, dates and names only: *Tuesday, office, Kaoru, please.* He did not stop until her thighs shook and her heel pressed a careful bruise into his shoulder and the rain lost track of its own minutes against the glass.

"Do not perform gratitude on municipal furniture," he said against her hip.

"I file gratitude in the break room," she gasped. "You said so. It is policy now. You set the precedent."

"Good." He rose, opened his belt, and she watched him hard and bare in the lamp light before he covered himself and her in the same unhurried order he did everything, the order of a man who planned even this and refused to be rushed through it. "Last deliverable. You ask for what the clip thinks it already saw."

"I ask." She pulled him down by the loosened collar of his open shirt, the deputy director undone to breath and hands. "Inside me. On the record. Deputy Director-sama."

He slid into her in one slow stroke that bowed her off the couch, full and stretched and choosing it with her whole disclosed self, the self that was now in a folder with her own name on it. He counted under his breath, one, two, three, and she met him, hips answering, the gold crane catching lamplight between their bodies. The couch was narrow and they did not care. He set a rhythm like a stamp coming down and coming down, deliberate, even, load-bearing, and when he told her to come on the word, she did, clenching around him with her face in his neck and the harbor lights smeared across the glass, and he followed deep with a groan he would deny in the morning and file, privately, where no portal could reach it, under *kept.*

After, he steadied her with his palm at her hip and said, again, that acceptable noise was still noise, which was as close as he came to telling her she had been loud and that he had liked it. He dealt with the practical filing he carried like a man who planned even this, and came back with a warm cloth and the blanket from the floor, and pulled her into the curve of his body so she could feel that the audit was not, technically, complete.

"You disclosed us," she said into his chest. "I am keeping the carbon copy."

"There is no carbon copy. There is a portal and my handwriting." His thumb traced her knuckle, slow, counting something he did not say aloud. "Both are harder to delete than a clip. That is the entire point. I chose the version of us that is difficult to erase."

---

The clip would loop until the channel found a louder one, a different muted official, a different mystery girl, a different eleven seconds. Toa peeled a clementine she had not asked permission to take and watched the rain keep its own minutes against the glass.

The file closed quieter than it had opened. Folder one and folder two were now one disclosed sentence the city had agreed to carry, reviewed by People & Conduct, accepted by the Council, oversight attached and signed for. The conflict had not been hidden. It had been declared, which the remix accounts hated, because the truth was boring and you could not dance to boring. Boring was enforceable. Boring did not get suspended or revoked at the next public hearing. Boring was, it turned out, a kind of safety she had spent her whole panic-bright life refusing on principle, mistaking it for the opposite of being alive.

"Pack up before the building decides we are a story it can stamp," Kaoru said, straightening his collar without a mirror, the charcoal suit reassembling itself around the deputy director the way it always did at the end of an audit.

"I will post *signatory conflict resolved in the margin* and let Sango translate. She has gotten very good at it. Last time she rendered *exclusive rider survived cross-examination* as two emojis and a clementine."

"Motion denied on translation." He held the door, deputy director fully reassembled, almost-smile filed for another quarter. "Go home, To-chan. Read the plate first."

She read the plate. It said his name, and his title, and the office that answered to the Council. She had been opening the right door for three cases now and pretending, every time, that she had wandered in by mistake.

She had not. Not once since the first.

---

*His side*

The clip was not a finding. It was a frame, and frames were the only weapon a port had that he could not stamp into compliance. He read the inbox the way he read a tide, by volume and direction, not by individual waves. People & Conduct opened folder one and folder two on the Council's instruction and asked him to explain the gap between the public narrative and his calendar, and he could have closed that gap in four lines and a recusal and never had to feel any of it.

He offered her the recusal honestly, laid it on the table beside the disclosure as an equal option, because bureaucracy without choice was tyranny, and tyranny made applicants lie on forms, and he had built an entire career on applicants who did not have to lie to him. Move the packet to Saito. Clean conflict. A defensible file, a stranger's chop, and the loss of the only honest reason he had to keep her in his afternoons. He told her all of that, including the last part, because she was the signatory's dependent and the file outlived her and she deserved the math.

She chose the margin. She chose disclosure, the riskier filing, the one that did not erase the conflict but declared it, the one that survived the clip by agreeing with it on their terms. He wrote it in his own hand because he did not trust a template with the sentence and did not want the board reading it in anyone's voice but his.

If he was honest, and he was, internally, with the brutal economy he spent on capacity charts, the recusal had stopped being an option for him in a rented studio with a piano half a step out of tune. He had sat down at it to certify a deliverable and ended up accompanying her, badly and then well, and a man who had once played the breath under a dancer's développé and waited for her balance to ask for the next chord could not, afterward, look the Council in the eye and call her a packet. The clause had always been the cover sheet. The piano had been the document. *Personal relationship. Disclosed. Oversight accepted.* The port owned it now. He had spent a career keeping his evenings off every ledger the Council could pull, and he had just signed one onto the permanent record on purpose, where a board of councilors could read it and rule on it and never once claim he had hidden it.

He took her on the office couch because she asked in writing with her whole body, and he counted because counting was the only verb his job title allowed for wanting. He had denied her in a service elevator, on this couch, in a harbor inn under western rain. He had run out of floors that were not on her calendar. The disclosure was the last door, the one with both their names finally on the plate, and he had read the plate and opened it anyway.

He did not tell her he had already drafted the response to the next thing, the parking garage rumor, the leaked-footage question that had not surfaced yet but would, because rumors in this port arrived on a schedule and he kept the schedule. He told her to go home and read the plate. That was scheduling. That was keeping. With him they were the same verb, and she had finally learned to read it.

The clip looped. The file closed. He left no row blank this time. He filled the one he had guarded for weeks with the truest sentence he owned, and that was the most he had ever disclosed to anyone, in handwriting, on letterhead, where the building could read it and the harbor could not.
