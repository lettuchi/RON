# Ryoko Owari Nights

### *The Left Hand of the Emerald Magistrate*

> An itinerant Crane dancer earns a corrupt magistrate's companion seal and follows him through five murders, believing she is only his witness, never guessing that he has been auditioning her for his left hand all along.

**Content note.** This is a slow-burn romance set in a corrupt canal city. It contains coercive power dynamics, an unjust legal system, implied off-page violence, and adult intimacy handled as fade to black. Nothing is shown that the lanterns would not politely look away from.

*A romantasy adaptation of the visual novel, canon route. Third person, mostly Toa's eyes, a few of his.*

---

## Prologue · The Wrong Door

*迷い戸 (the door she mistook)*

Lantern light pooled on the polished floorboards, and every sliding door looked the same.

Toa had counted four of them, then six, then stopped counting, because counting was beginning to feel like an admission. The noble quarter of Ryoko Owari smelled of ink and cedar and, underneath that, of canal water gone faintly wrong, a green smell that did not belong to clean rivers. She clutched her permit book against her chest with one hand and her hanko case with the other, and she turned down a corridor that refused to end.

"I was sure the clerk said third hall, fourth door on the left," she murmured. "Or was it the right."

Her own voice came back too small. Somewhere behind these walls a man held the seal she needed, and the city would not let her perform a single step until his red wax said she could. That was the shape of the thing. She had crossed provinces for ink. She had left the Unicorn roads and the caravan that never warned her about local renewals, and now the ink lived behind a door she could not find.

She squared her shoulders the way her mother had taught her, spine first, always the spine first. "One more corridor," she told the empty hall. "Then I ask nicely. Very nicely."

The door she chose slid open under her knock before she had finished knocking, and a voice came out of the warm dark of the room like a blade drawn slowly from a sheath.

"Done complaining?"

Toa startled back a step. A man sat behind a low desk inside, half in lamplight: brown hair slicked back from a high forehead, robes of gold and deep maroon, and grey eyes that found her and did not hurry over her. He looked at her the way a clerk looks at a line he intends to cross out.

"I wasn't," she said, and then heard how it sounded, and bowed too fast. "That is, I apologize for the disturbance, sochira-no-kata."

His mouth did something that was not a smile. "Wrong door. Again."

The screen shut. Not slammed, exactly. Closed with the finality of a verdict, with a soft wooden click that rang in her ears longer than any slam would have. Toa stood in the corridor with her permit book and her pride and the dawning, infuriating certainty that the man behind that door was precisely the man she had come to find.

She left, because leaving felt like dignity. Three offices later a junior clerk squinted at her dates and pointed her back the way she had come.

"The Emerald Magistrate," the clerk said, and there was something careful in how he said it. "That door stamps interclan renewals. Good luck. He eats petitioners for breakfast."

"I've come too far," Toa said, "to be scared off by a reputation."

The corridor looped, of course it looped, and deposited her exactly where she had started, in front of the same screen, with the same wrong-green smell threading up from the canal below. She breathed. She folded her hands. She knelt at the threshold and struck the frame twice, crisp and measured, the way a petitioner knocks when she has decided to stop being a nuisance and start being a guest.

This time he let her in.

---

The office past the screen was a museum of other people's trouble. Shelves climbed the walls, stacked with case files and curios: a Lion mon here, an Emerald seal there, scrolls bound in cords whose colors she could read like a map of the man's enemies. She kept her eyes on the desk. It was harder than it should have been.

"Better," he said, watching her not look. "Most Crane can't resist cataloging a room like a museum."

"Magistrate, I need my permit renewed." She chose the plain words, because the plain words were true and because she suspected he would respect them more than a flourish. "I have the paperwork."

"Plain speech." He set down the cup of water at his elbow without drinking from it. The cup, she would notice later, never seemed to move except when he wished it to. "You waste less of my evening than most. Names and purpose. Quickly."

"Kakita Toa. Dancer of the Kakita family, new to Ryoko Owari. Post-gempukku, traveling from Unicorn lands. I mean to establish residence and perform legally."

"Crane hair, Unicorn permit dates." His grey gaze touched the white of her hair, the white of her lashes, the gold crane worked into her obi, and moved on as if filing each item under a separate heading. "Wrong clan for this desk. Wrong week to fix it. Emerald Magistrate Kitsu Kaoru. You will use my title if you wish to leave with a seal."

"Forgive me, Emerald Magistrate."

"Door," he said, and gestured, and the screen slid shut behind her at a flick of his fingers, sealing them in with the lamp and the cedar and the cup of water that waited on the desk like a witness. He nodded at a cushion already set on the tatami at his knee, and tapped it once. "Cushion. Floor. You stand too much for someone begging favors."

She sat. She set her permit book and hanko case on the desk, close enough to show, not close enough to surrender.

He read the dates. She watched his face do nothing, which was its own kind of language.

"These dates," he said.

"They should be current. I checked before leaving."

"They are not. Your permit expired. Unicorn bureaucracy failed to mention it?" He let the silence work. "If you cannot renew locally, the law sends you back to the Academy for reissue."

The floor seemed to tilt. The Academy meant the long road back, and the long road back meant arriving in disgrace with nothing to show for the leaving. "There must be a remedy short of exile," she said, and was proud that her voice held. "Emerald Magistrate, what do you require?"

Something flickered in him then, a small satisfaction, quickly housed. "Requirements. Finally, a useful word. Most petitioners wail. You ask for terms. That almost earns respect."

"Almost is a start." She made herself smile. "I dance for a living. I know how to build toward a finale."

That was when she understood the shape of the evening. He leaned back. He told her, without ever saying the words outright, that the seal would cost a performance. Dance listed on her form, venue missing, patron column blank: do the math, he said, and she did, and the math came out to her, on her feet, in his office, after dark.

"Impress me," he said.

She could have refused. She thought about refusing for exactly as long as it took to remember the gate that closed at midnight and the canal district that waited beyond it for women without paper. Pride was a costume. Tonight she would wear a different one.

"If I perform," she said, "you sign. No delays. No new conditions after."

"Bold." A thread of charm warmed his voice, there and gone. "I sign when I'm satisfied, not when you're tired. That is the only term."

"Then I'll dance until you're satisfied, or until dawn breaks my ankles."

"Acceptable. Begin."

---

The lamp shrank the room to the size of the two of them. Toa loosened her obi a careful degree, not indecent, only deliberate, and let the silk breathe, and unfolded her fan, and stepped into the only thing she had ever been entirely sure of.

She danced the maiden at the gate. The road is long, the steps said. She walks it anyway. The obijime bells at her waist whispered at each turn, and the fan opened and closed like a held breath, and she told a small story with her whole body because the body was the only honest argument she had ever owned. He watched, still as carved stone. Once, near the middle of it, he reached for the cup and drank, slowly, his eyes never leaving her.

"Better," he said. "Keep going. I haven't touched the hanko yet."

So she kept going. The maiden's story ran past its ending and into a second movement she invented on the spot, every turn aimed at the desk, at the man, at the seal he would not lift. When the fan finally closed she did not bow. She held the last breath, the way she had been taught to make an audience lean forward without knowing why.

"That earns your seal," she said. "You said impress you. I did."

"Merit enough." He let it hang. "Signature. Pending."

"Pending."

"Continue."

She felt the heat climb her throat, anger and something less easy to name braided together. He was not going to make this clean. He was never going to make anything clean. She danced on, softer now, the bells gone quiet, the coquette's sway sharpened into something that was half defiance and half question, and his cup came to rest on the desk, and his eyes did not blink.

"A good performance," he said. And then, lower: "Better when you stop pretending the room is empty."

His hand found her wrist. Not painful. Not gentle either. Entitlement dressed up as appraisal, the grip of a man who had decided the distance between them was his to set.

She had a choice in that grip, and she felt it land in her chest like a coin dropped down a well. She could rebuke him, pull free, finish the dance as defiance. Or she could not pull away at all.

She did not pull away.

The dance used his hold as counterweight, dangerous and deliberate, her own balance given over to his hand and taken back on her own terms. "If you're going to hold me," she heard herself say, low, "hold me like you mean the audition."

"Better," he breathed.

The chair creaked as she shifted her weight to its arm, close enough now that she could see the grey of his eyes was the exact grey of her own, a thing she would think about later and wish she had not. Two figures, one lamp, one question hanging in the cedar-scented dark.

"Unless?" he said.

She understood the word the way she understood a final beat of music: it was an opening, and openings were for stepping into.

She closed the distance.

---

What followed stayed between petitioner and magistrate. The latch caught. The lamplight thinned to amber. His hand found her wrist again, and this time it was not restraint but invitation, drawing her off the chair and into the narrow warm space between the desk and the lantern. Negotiation dissolved into breath. Her fingers found his collar; his thumb traced the line of her jaw; the gold cord came loose under her hands slowly, because she did not rush, and he had watched her long enough to know she would not.

"Magistrate-sama," she said, and meant it as more than a title.

"Magistrate-sama stays on your tongue," he answered, "for what comes next."

They folded down onto the cushion already waiting on the tatami, and the lamp painted them in gold and shadow, and the law on the desk above them went quiet for a while. It was not entered in any clerk's ledger. It was only theirs, the implied weight of it, the heat where the paperwork used to sit.

When breath and silk found their order again, robes loose and cheeks flushed, the office smelled of ink and cedar and not of scandal. He drew the renewal from her permit book. The hanko rose, law made weight in red wax, and pressed once, crisp, against the page.

"There," he said. "Signed."

She reached for it, dizzy with relief and with other things. Then she held the paper to the lamp and read the date he had written, and the relief went strange in her hands.

"This date," she said. "You dated it forward. Three days."

"Effective when I say." He was already arranging his collar, already the magistrate again, the warmth folded back into its box. "Return on the thirtieth. We'll discuss your residency then."

She laughed, because the alternative was something less composed. He had given her exactly what she came for and arranged it so she would have to come back to receive it. "Guest room preview," she said. "Forward-dated paperwork. And you call it negotiable."

"Bring obedience," he said. "The rest is negotiable. The thirtieth. You'll knock on the right door. I'll leave it unlatched."

She gathered her permit book and her hanko case and her composure, in that order, and let herself out into the corridor where the air was colder than the office. Somewhere a bell marked an hour that was not yet dawn. Three days. She could survive three days on curry and pride. She had survived worse on less.

What she could not do, walking the looping corridor back toward the gate with the wrong-green smell rising off the canal, was stop turning the moment over in her hands like a coin she could not quite spend: the grey eyes that matched her own, the cup that never moved until he wished it to, the seal he had pressed into her future and then dated three days out of her reach.

Snacks first had been the plan. Pride second. Justice somewhere after that.

She had gotten the order wrong, and she could not, for the life of her, decide if she minded.

## I · Appointment of a Live-In Companion

*居候の印 (the seal of keeping)*

She came back on the morning of the thirtieth, which is to say she came back early, three days early, with tea and a borrowed case file and the particular brightness of a woman who has decided to be useful before anyone asks.

The noble quarter tasted of ink and cedar in the daylight too. Toa knelt at the threshold of the right door, the door she now knew by heart, and waited to be invited, because waiting had worked before.

"The permit says I'm legal on the thirtieth," she said, when the screen slid back. "I'm early. With tea, and a case file."

Kitsu Kaoru looked up from his desk and did not pretend to be surprised. "Early is still obedience," he said. "Sit before you spill something on my docket."

The cushion waited in the same place, the place where he had made her sit for her audition, the place where everything else had happened after. She sat anyway. She poured the tea anyway. Pride, she was learning, could be folded as neatly as linen if you stopped trying to wear it every minute of the day.

"Snacks after paperwork," she said. "That's still the order of operations, isn't it."

"We'll see if your appetite survives what I'm about to read you aloud." He let her settle. Then, without warmth and without warning, in the flat tone of a man recording a fact: "I like you. Enough that I want to keep you around."

She nearly dropped the pot.

He did not look up to enjoy it, which was somehow worse. He slid a stack of stationery across the desk, not her permit book this time but office paper stamped with the Emerald crest, and the top sheet bore a title in his blunt vertical hand, and her own name inked beneath it as though it had been settled long before she walked in.

*Appointment of Live-In Companion. Patronage of Artistic Residence.*

"A guest room," she said slowly. "A preview. You really do enjoy making people come back."

"The signature was always going to bring you back. I merely wrote out the appointment you'd earned." He turned the page so she could read the rest. "Before I read it aloud, one question. Plainly. When your permit was dying, you danced for me in this room. The quarter forgets dancers who vanish after sunset. I don't."

"You watched like the whole city had gone quiet." She heard her own honesty arrive before she could dress it. "That was not a clerk's audition, Magistrate-sama."

"No. Witnesses lie. You had a body worth housing and a voice worth putting on a filing." He said it the way another man might compliment a horse, and she felt the insult and the want tangled together in it, and could not have said which one warmed her more. "Ask me what I want from you. The version you write down on paper, not the polite one."

So she did. "What do you actually want from me?"

"Attendance. Discretion. Art that doesn't embarrass the seal on my door." He tapped the sheet. "I'm drafting sponsorship, not a courtesan's contract. The ledger will read art donor to artist. It would insult your lord and mine both if I played danna to a Crane who crossed provinces for ink."

"Sponsorship," she repeated, and felt the word settle somewhere between relief and a disappointment she did not examine. "Not. Not that."

"Gifts of money to support your artistic expression. I know exactly what I'm paying for." His grey eyes held hers a beat too long. "Interpret that liberally, within the residence I set."

He read her the duties then, in the voice he used for law because, he said, it was law. Attend the magistrate. Carry messages. Witness interviews. Keep discretion. Remain on the premises unless he released her. Take meals in the inner hall. Sleep in the chamber adjoining his office. Answer when he called, day or night.

"Day or night," she said. "You ask a great deal, Magistrate-sama."

"It sounds like law because it is. Ryoko Owari doesn't grant interclan residence to dancers who vanish after sunset." He tapped a second page, where the numbers lived: stipend figures, duty rotations, a line for her seal beside his. "Three koku monthly, plus board. Official gifts to support your expression. The treasury won't call you a kept performer so long as the ledger says patronage."

Three koku. It was more than the curry shop had ever paid her to smile at tourists, more than she had carried in her whole life at once. She thought of the pony that needed shoes and the innkeeper who tolerated her for folding linens, and she understood the bargain, all of it, the room and the rank and the rope dressed up as ribbon.

She could have negotiated. She knew how. She had built a whole life out of asking for terms. But she looked at her name already inked in his hand, at the chamber waiting past the inner screen, at the man who had dated her future three days forward just to make sure she returned, and she found that the thing she wanted most was not leverage.

"Magistrate-sama," she said, and made her voice steady, the sponsored artist she meant to become. "I accept the appointment and its duties. You'll have my discretion and my attendance."

"Better." Something moved through him, satisfaction housed quickly behind the cold. "You almost sound like you belong behind my desk, instead of on it."

She took up the brush. Her seal met his on the line, waxless, official, irrevocable until he said otherwise. He pressed his chop beside it, and the small wooden sound of it was the sound of a door closing on the life she had been living and opening on the one she had not yet learned to name.

"Effective on the thirtieth, as written," he said. "Until then, you're mine to schedule. Starting with Case One."

"Case One it is." She gathered herself. "Files, witnesses, and snacks for the road."

"You'll fetch the docket from the inner archive. Then you'll learn how this city buries its dead without funerals."

She rose, and asked where the chamber was, because some part of her wanted to set down her belongings in a room of her own before the work swallowed her whole.

"Past the inner screen, down the left corridor," he said. "Don't rearrange my shelves."

"I only peeked the once."

"I notice everything. Unpack after the docket, not before."

He told her the rest at the inner screen, where the stairs went down into the smell of old paper and river damp. Five matters on his slate this week, he said. Companion ink put her on his witness docket for all of them, Case One through Five. Survive all five, or the quarter ate her stipend.

"The permit was for dance," she said, and heard the surprise in her own mouth. "Patronage. A room behind your screen. Not five corpses in rotation."

"Dance bought you the corridor. Cases buy your keep. Competence within reach. That's what I want." He nodded at the dark stair. "Archive stairs. Case One ledger. Before the watch blanks the line. When you come back, we open the file on the canal body. No audience. No bells. Only answers."

"Snacks after paperwork," she said. "The rule still holds. Even for corpses without funerals."

"Bring rice crackers. I'll allow one indulgence before the city disappoints you."

She went down into the dark with a lantern and a list, and she did not see the way he watched her go.

---

*He kept files on everyone.*

It was the only honest religion the city had left him, and Kaoru practiced it the way other men practiced devotion: a name, a line, a column for what each person would do when frightened and what they would do when they thought no one was filing.

The dancer had a thick file now, though she would have been alarmed to learn it. Wrong door, twice. Plain speech under pressure. A bow that did not wobble. A spine her mother had clearly set before the Unicorn roads could bend it. She had asked for terms instead of weeping, and she had read the forward date and laughed instead of cursing him, and both of those went into the column he did not have a polite name for.

He turned the signed appointment toward the lamp. *Live-In Companion. Patronage of Artistic Residence.* It was a true document. It was also a smaller word than the one he meant, the way a guest room was a smaller word than what he was building toward, room by room, case by case.

The city would need a left hand before winter. A name it could cite when the seal walked, a yoriki sworn to the Emerald office, someone who could read a lie at the Lacquered Plum and not run when the comb turned out to be Scorpion. He had been looking for that name for two years and finding only clerks who sold their courage by the cup.

He did not write the word down. He never wrote a verdict before the evidence closed; that was how men ended up forging their own seals. But he watched the stairwell where her lantern light had gone, and he let himself note, in the private column no clerk would ever audit, that the audition had begun, and that the candidate did not yet know she was auditioning, and that this was exactly how he preferred to run it.

"Snacks after paperwork," he said to the empty office, and almost, almost, was amused.

## II · The Borrowed Face

*借り物の顔 (Case One: the canal body)*

The archive stairs swallowed every footfall, and by the time Toa climbed back into the light she had dust in her hakama and a ledger under her arm that smelled of river damp and ink old enough to flake. She had not let go of it once. Small victories. No bells on her, obijime dry, the docket intact: she had decided to count those as wins, because the alternative was to count the things she did not yet understand, and that list was longer.

He was waiting in the corridor when she reached the top, as if he had known to the breath how long the archive would take.

"You took long enough," he said. "Set the docket on my desk and try not to breathe on the wax seals."

"You said archive dust was acceptable. I assume my breathing counts too."

In the office he broke the outer seal with his thumbnail and spread the case across the cedar: watch reports, a charcoal sketch of a narrow cut between warehouses, and a line at the top left blank, waiting for someone to put a face to the dead.

"I unpacked for patronage," she said, looking at the blank line. "You're handing me a corpse."

"Patronage keeps you in my corridor. Case One opens on my desk. First of five." He turned the sketch toward her. "A body in the canal at first light. Noble quarter, where the water runs clean enough to mirror gold leaf. The watch writes drowning whenever the name line stays empty. You'll learn which boxes they'll sign, and which they won't."

"Clean water, dirty ledger." She studied the sketch, the rope marks scored into stone. "Who pulled him out?"

"Night watch. Fishermen who swore they saw nothing. And the Scorpion quarter upstream pretends the current runs backward." He tapped the report. "The victim wears Miya colors under a borrowed haori. Retainer's knot. Someone wanted him found. Someone else wanted him lost."

"Miya means Crane politics," she said. "And Crane politics means you, Magistrate-sama."

"Which is exactly why you sleep in the room beside my office. Discretion isn't decoration." He pushed the victim summary at her. "Study it. Tell me what the watch missed."

She bent over the page, and the world narrowed to it, the way it always did when a body was the question. The Academy had trained her hands and her feet, but the gift underneath the training was older and stranger and entirely her own: she read bodies the way other people read weather. She could not turn it off.

"Retainer's knot," she said, "tied left-handed. The haori is two sizes too wide. Borrowed, like you said, or put on him after. No wallet. No letter case." Her finger stopped at a clerk's margin note. "And his fingernails were scrubbed clean the night before. That's labor without the honest dirt that should come with it."

Kaoru went still in the particular way he did when she had surprised him and did not intend to admit it. "Good. The watch wrote drowning and missed half of what you just said." He rose. "Then we go to the water. Stand where I point you. Don't compose."

---

They left through the inner hall, not the public corridor where she had once wandered lost. The guards nodded to Kaoru and looked straight through her, the way one looks through furniture that has somehow learned to walk.

"Furniture with legs still memorizes the route," she murmured, and if he heard her, he chose not to dignify it.

Ryoko Owari woke around them as they walked: cart wheels, a shamisen somewhere distant, sweet smoke threading out of a vial house two alleys over. And under all of it, the thing she had been trying not to name since the prologue, the thinness in the air.

"It's wrong here," she said. "The air. Thin, like someone peeled a ward off the wall and left bare paper behind."

"Wrong-colored water usually means a clerk poured something upstream." He did not slow. "Check the eaves before you blame charms. Thieves in the noble quarter work for employers, not hunger."

On a warehouse sign above them sat a crow, painted, or perhaps real, she could not tell in the grey light, marked with three eyes that were not quite Imperial and not quite Scorpion and not quite anything she could name. It watched them. When Kaoru glanced up, it took wing, and she told herself the cold she felt was only the river.

The canal, when they reached it, ran black-green under the morning cloud. Greener than honest water. As if someone had dissolved a charm and tipped the dregs downstream. Rope burns scarred the stone where the watch had hauled the body up. There was no crowd, only a clerk with a wax tablet and a Miya envoy who stood reading a warehouse sign with great concentration so that he would not have to look at the pallet.

"To-chan." It was the first time he had used the name since the office, and it landed soft and proprietary in the cold. "Stand where I point you. Don't touch the water."

The clerk folded back the damp sheet. A young man's face showed, peaceful in entirely the wrong way, as if his sleep had been negotiated rather than earned.

"He looks borrowed," she said quietly. "Even now."

"Identification first. Gossip a distant second. Mourning never." But he let her look. He had brought her here to look.

She read the hands. She could not help it; they told her everything. "Palms soft until yesterday. Ink on the index finger, here, ledger work. And the sleeve hem, the slime on it is from upstream, not from this stretch of the canal." She straightened, and the cold went all the way through her. "He didn't drown here, Magistrate-sama. He died somewhere else, and then someone dressed him for display."

"Which means," Kaoru said, "someone wanted him found a particular way." He turned to her, and his next words were a test wearing the shape of an instruction. "Summarize it. One sentence. If I say your words back to the Crane envoy, does he flinch, or does he laugh?"

She felt the trap in it. The eager answer rose in her throat, the whole theory, dressed for display the way the body had been, and she swallowed it, because she had begun to understand the game he was teaching her without ever calling it a lesson.

"Recovery at mark three," she said instead, flat and clean. "Clothing as listed. Cause of death pending your review, Magistrate-sama. The envoy won't laugh. He won't fear me either. The rest, I'll say where the walls don't lean in to listen."

His mouth moved, almost. "Safe," he said. "You're learning when to keep the blade sheathed. Keep learning."

It was near the rope post that the lacquer caught the light. Something small, wedged in the seam where oil and wrong-colored water met. She saw it before he did, and for once she had the sense not to reach for it.

"A comb," she said. "Scorpion mon inlaid. Not Miya."

"Don't touch it with bare hands." He wrapped it himself in wax cloth, eyes never leaving the eaves, and handed it to her only long enough for her seal on the chain-of-custody line, as if even her gloved fingers were a risk he was logging. Then the clerk lifted the separate haori from a dry crate, and in the lining the wax had melted, but the inner fold still held a courier's chit, half legible under river stain.

She read it aloud before she could stop herself, and this time the name was worth saying. "Miya Jiro. Guest-house ledger clerk. *Deliver before third bell.*" She looked up. "That's a name now, Magistrate-sama. Not a rumor."

"Jiro handled Crane correspondence for the noble-quarter guest house." Kaoru's gaze had gone somewhere cold and far. "Someone killed the clerk and still kept the appointment."

Across the canal, the Miya envoy finally looked up from the warehouse sign. He saw the chit in Kaoru's hand, and remembered urgent business elsewhere, and was gone before the clerk had finished writing.

---

Back among cedar and ink, the Scorpion comb sat in a lacquer tray like a poisonous jewel, and Kaoru drew his brush across the line the watch had left blank.

*Miya Jiro.*

"Tomorrow we cross the Miya guest registry against Scorpion shipping rolls," he said. "One of them flinches the moment Jiro's name is said aloud."

Toa watched the ink dry into the page. A guest-house clerk, a Crane thread, a Scorpion comb. "Someone staged a whole poem here," she said. "And forgot it would have an audience."

"Eat." He pushed the bag of rice crackers across the desk, her indulgence, his allowance. "Then copy the watch testimony twice. Once for the Empire. Once for what I actually believe happened."

She took a cracker, because the rule held even now, especially now, and she looked at the name on the line and made herself a small private promise, the kind that does not go in any ledger.

"Case One has a face," she said. "Jiro. I won't forget him."

"No," Kaoru agreed, and something in how he said it made her glance up, but his face had already closed. "I don't expect you will. That is rather the point of you."

She did not know yet what he meant. She only knew that the corpse had a name now, and that she was glad of the room next to his, and that the wrong-green water outside the window was still rising.

## III · The Lacquered Plum

*漆の梅 (Case One: the river room)*

By morning the docket had grown teeth. Beside Jiro's name Kaoru had pinned a second sheet: the Miya guest house, the Lacquered Plum, where a dead clerk had once kept other men's appointments, deep in the licensed quarter where the okami answered to coin and not to the watch.

"She won't open her registry for a watchman," Kaoru said. "She might open it for a magistrate. Bring my seal. You walk a half step behind me, and you speak only when I open the door. The quarter swallows companions who forget who lights the lanterns."

Toa thought about that, and about the three years of her life she had spent carrying tea in painted rooms much like the one they were walking toward, and she made her offer carefully.

"Send me in through the kitchen first," she said. "I spent those years smiling at tired women in rooms like this. They'll tell a dancer what they'd never tell your seal. Go in soft, I mean. You follow once the room is warm."

Something crossed his face that was not quite displeasure. "Your past, turned into a bargaining chip," he said. "I dislike how useful it keeps being." A beat. "Go in soft. I'll follow. Don't enjoy it more than the case."

---

Rain reached the licensed quarter first. It beaded on paper lanterns the color of old wine and ran the canal black between teahouses that sold forgetting by the hour. The Lacquered Plum leaned out over the water on cedar stilts, and on the gable perched the three-eyed crow again, the same painted sign or a cousin of it, and this time it did not fly. The air around the doorway was thin in the way she had learned to dread, peeled-charm thin, the protection scraped off and never replaced.

"Or never wanted," Kaoru said, when she said so. "The Plum prefers guests unwatched by anything that reports upward."

Inside it was all lamplight and face powder. The okami knelt behind a low desk stacked with appointment books, her smile fixed hard as lacquer, her eyes already tallying the cost of a magistrate's boots on her tatami. She offered them the river's version of the story before they had finished crossing the threshold: Jiro drowned, a sad and ordinary thing, drunk boys and high water, the tale the canal wrote every season.

While she said it, Toa was already gone, slipped past the desk the kitchen way, a servant's bow and the easy warmth of a woman who had carried tea in worse rooms. In the corner a young geisha looked up from folding a dead man's spare haori, white makeup cried through at the edges, perhaps nineteen years old and bowing too low, the way frightened people bow once they have already decided to be brave.

"You knew him," Toa said softly. "Jiro. I'm not here to turn his name into a scandal. I'm here so the river doesn't get the last word."

"I'm Suzu." The girl's voice shook and held. "Jiro brought me persimmons on the nights the patrons were cruel. He didn't drown, Magistrate-sama." This last to Kaoru, who had come in soft behind Toa exactly as promised, his stillness filling the room more than any shout. "He was afraid for a whole week before the canal took him."

"Afraid of what?" Kaoru asked.

"A guest. One who books the river room under a flower name and tips in foreign coin. Jiro carried letters into that room and came back grey."

Kaoru tilted the wrapped comb toward the lamp, and Suzu went the color of her own powder.

"It's his," she whispered. "The flower-name guest. He left it in the river room a month ago and threatened to flay the maid who'd dared touch it. Scorpion mon, three lacquer scales. I'd know it in the dark."

The girl was a frightened thing with one true thing left in her mouth, and Toa knew exactly how that felt, and knew that fear would not keep it there long. So she knelt, all the way down, dancer to dancer, until her eyes were below Suzu's and the girl had to look down to find them.

"Suzu. Sit. Breathe." She kept her hands where they could be watched, open, slow. "You're not betraying him by saying it. You're the only funeral he's ever going to get."

It was the kitchen bow that did it, the kneeling, the dancer instead of the seal. The girl had already decided Toa was safe, and now her words came in a rush, like water finding the crack it had wanted all along.

"They call him the Chrysanthemum. The river room is always his on high-tide nights. Jiro read his letters before he carried them. He found a barge manifest that named the wrong cargo, and he meant to take it to a magistrate he trusted." Suzu's breath hitched. "Then the tide came in."

"A flower-named Scorpion," Toa said. "A barge carrying a lying manifest. And a clerk who tried to do the honorable thing." She felt the grief and the anger braid together in her chest. "Jiro had a spine after all."

---

The okami surrendered the high-tide registry the way a gambler gives up a marked card, slowly, watching the door the whole time. Kaoru turned to the night Jiro died, and there it was: the river room, reserved under the Chrysanthemum, paid a full season ahead. And there, in a dead man's own careful hand, the third-bell delivery, signed off the very night the canal took him.

The booking was authorized in red. A private-room writ, the kind the licensed quarter demanded of the magistracy to seal a room against the watch, and the wax bore the Emerald mon.

Toa leaned close, and the gift in her hands woke, the one that read the small wrong things. "Magistrate-sama. Look at the seal." She did not raise her voice; the walls leaned in. "This chrysanthemum has sixteen petals. The Emerald office only ever cuts fourteen. The stamp's a forgery. A fine one. But the petal count gives it away."

Kaoru's stillness changed temperature. "Sixteen petals," he said, very quietly. "Someone carved a false Emerald seal to lock a room I never authorized, on the very night a Crane clerk was strangled inside my jurisdiction."

The screen slid open without a knock. A man stepped in from the rain in Scorpion grey, a trade factor by his ink-stained cuffs and the easy menace of someone used to buying magistrates by the dozen.

"The Plum's books are the commercial records of a chartered shipping concern," he said. "Privileged. You'll hand them to me, Magistrate, and forget the flower growing in them."

"I am the privilege in this city." Kaoru did not move, and somehow his not moving was worse than if he had. "Take one step toward that registry, and the watch fishes a second body out of the noble quarter by dawn."

"The watch reports drowning. The watch reports whatever keeps the tax barges moving. You're one seal among many, Kitsu-san."

It was Toa who answered, because she could not stand the lie of it. "One seal stands between you and that book," she said, "and yet here you are, come to fetch it yourself. A truly privileged record wouldn't need collecting by a man who tips in foreign silver."

The factor's hand drifted toward his sleeve. Kaoru did not so much as glance at it. "The book stays," he said. "What we do with the flower's forged writ, that's your choice. Decide it before our guest decides for us."

She had a heartbeat to choose, and she chose patience over theater. "I'll trace the petals onto rice paper," she said, low, for Kaoru alone, "and set the book back exactly where it lay. Let whoever cut that false seal sleep easy one more night. Just long enough to lead us home."

"Patience over theater," Kaoru murmured, and there was approval in it, real and rare. "The Chrysanthemum keeps his appointment. We keep the advantage. Good."

He let the factor weigh it all: the room, the unmoving magistrate, the witness who had not flinched. "You came for a book and found a magistrate instead," Kaoru told him. "Tell the Chrysanthemum the Emerald office is missing a seal, and that I've decided to take an interest in flowers."

The factor bowed to the exact depth that insults without inviting a blade, and withdrew into the rain. "High tide waits for no one, Kitsu-san," he said at the screen. "Neither does the barge."

When the door slid shut, Suzu let out a breath she had held since he entered, and the okami had already begun deciding which version of the night she would sell, and to whom.

---

Back among cedar and ink, the case finally had a shape. On the lacquer tray: the Scorpion comb, Suzu's name, and the careful tracing of a forged Emerald writ.

"A barge that names false cargo," Kaoru said, low. "A Scorpion who books his rooms under a flower and tips in foreign silver. A clerk who tried to be honest and was strangled for the courtesy. And a seal cut to wear my mark, sealing the very room he died in." He set the tracing beside the comb. "Someone wants this murder to carry the Emerald crest."

He crossed the office and stopped too close, with the practiced ease of a man who had decided the distance between them was his to set. Toa held her ground, which she had learned was its own kind of conversation with him.

"You stood behind me all night," he said, "and let the quarter break itself against my stillness. That is exactly what a companion of mine is for."

"Behind you. Watching." She lifted her chin. "And I still saw the petal count before you did, Magistrate-sama."

His eyes moved over her face, slow, cataloging. "You did. Obedient and observant both." A pause she felt in her ribs. "I'm beginning to think I kept the right dancer."

It should not have warmed her the way it did. *Kept.* It was a word for objects and rooms and dancers on a ledger line. She told herself it was professional pride, the satisfaction of a witness whose witnessing had landed. She told herself that, and her face stayed pleasant, and underneath it something turned over and would not lie back down.

"The Scorpion shipping rolls list one barge on the next high tide," Kaoru went on, already the magistrate again, "booked to the same chartered concern as the river room. The flower will be aboard, or his manifest will. Either way, we meet the tide."

"And we copied it quiet," she said, finding the thread again. "The flower books his room one more high tide, never dreaming we traced his petals." She looked at the tray, at the comb and the name and the tracing. "Case One has a name, a comb, and a tide now. And I still won't forget Jiro."

He did not answer that. He was watching her across the lamplit desk with an expression she had not yet learned to read, the one that came over him when he had decided something and was not ready to file it where she could see.

Outside, the rain thickened, and the tide began, somewhere down in the dark, to climb.

## IV · Lanterns on Black Water

*赤い糸の灯 (interlude)*

The rain had thinned by the time they left the Plum, gentled to a mist that haloed the paper lanterns along the canal path. The water ran black under them, amber-streaked where the lantern light fell, and Toa caught the wrong-green smell again, the same she had breathed at Jiro's recovery site, and thought how strange it was that a city could be lovely and poisoned in the same breath.

"You read Suzu without breaking her," Kaoru said. He was not looking at the water. He was looking at her. "You read the forged petals before I finished my sentence. That is trust. And trust is rarer than obedience in this hall."

"Magistrate-sama." She kept her eyes on the path. "Case One isn't finished."

"Case One will still be there when you stop pretending the tide is the only clock that matters."

He walked a half step beside her. Not ahead. She noticed it the way she noticed everything, with her body before her mind caught up: he had set himself level with her, the magistrate who arranged all distances, and the wind shifted and her sleeve caught against his cuff and he did not pull away. No seal between them now. No clerk, no envoy, no okami counting the cost of their boots. Only the lantern stripes laid across the black water, and the two of them walking through them.

"If the watch sees," she began.

"The watch files what I sign. Tonight I sign nothing." His voice was low and even. "Walk."

So she walked, and let her sleeve stay where it was, and tried not to make a charter out of a sleeve.

It was a hard thing to hold in her hands, what this was. She turned it over as they climbed back toward the compound, the way she turned over a coin she could not spend. The contract on his desk read *art donor to artist*. Three koku and board and a chamber adjoining his office. He had told her plainly, twice, that he knew exactly what he was paying for, and she had signed it with her eyes open, the way she signed everything, because she had crossed provinces for a place to stand and this was the place. That was the shape of it. Patronage. A schedule. A hall and a room and a man who arranged distances.

And yet he had set himself a half step beside her in the dark where no ledger could see, and that small thing undid more of her composure than the whole of the prologue had.

---

The inner screen was locked when they reached the office; he locked it himself. The comb and the traced writ waited on the lacquer tray, and he crossed to the lamps and dimmed them, one by one, until the room glowed the same amber as the canal they had walked beside.

"You asked me to stay where you could see me," he said. "At the registry. I am not in the habit of granting dangerous requests twice."

His fingers found her knuckle. Not a clerk's grip, the one she had learned to brace for, the one that found a pulse the way he found a misplaced line. Not a threat. Just his hand, deliberate and warm, closing over hers, and her breath answered him before her courtesy could remember it was supposed to object.

"Sign nothing tonight," she said. The words came out smaller than she meant and more honest. "Just. Don't send me to the barge alone."

Something in him eased, or sharpened, she could never tell which, in him they were so often the same motion. "A dangerous request," he said, "from my companion. I'll allow it until the wax cools."

He drew her in. And whatever else Kitsu Kaoru was, whatever cruelty he wore by daylight and signed into the ledgers of a city that deserved no better, here in the amber dark his cruelty softened into something that still took, but let her choose the giving. He had not stopped being the man who arranged distances. He had only, for one night, arranged this one to nothing.

What followed stayed off every ledger. Implied, never filed: the loosening of an obi that no clerk would record, the heat where the law usually sat, his hands in her white hair and her mouth at his collar until words gave out entirely. The lamp burned low. The rain returned to the eaves, soft as a held breath, and the city went on lying to itself outside the locked screen, and inside it, for a while, no one lied at all.

When they dressed, the rain had thickened again, and somewhere downstream the barge waited on the climbing tide.

"Snacks after tides," he said, fitting his collar back into its severe lines, the magistrate reassembling himself piece by piece. "I expect you fed before the canal tries to drown you."

"I ate already." She smiled at the floor, because if she smiled at him he would read too much, and she would let him. "Don't fuss."

He did not fuss. He never fussed. But at the screen, before he slid it back on the corridor and the work and the rest of the terrible night, he paused with his hand on the frame and looked at her for a moment longer than the schedule required, and did not say whatever it was, and went out into the rain.

She stood alone in the amber room with the comb and the traced writ and the warmth still on her skin, and she did not let herself name the thing that ached. There was no line for it on any form she had signed. She was his sponsored artist, his witness, his companion in the room adjoining his office. That was the shape of it.

She told herself that, and went to find her wakizashi, because the tide would not wait, and neither, she was learning, would he.

---

## V · High Tide

*潮の帳 (Case One: the barge)*

The noble quarter at high tide smelled of wet rope and lamp oil. Warehouses seemed to kneel into the canal, and one low barge, painted Scorpion grey, waited where the factor had promised it would, gangplank lifted on water that climbed by the finger's width every time she blinked.

Kaoru stood at the quay in magistrate black, seal in one hand, the traced writ folded in the other. Rain beaded on his collar. He did not look at her when she came up beside him with her wakizashi tied correct for witness work, only at the barge, as if the whole chartered lie were a clerk he intended to intimidate into truth.

"Chartered concern, false cargo, a flower name on the ledger," he said. "Tonight we seize the manifest before the tide signs off on another drowning."

"Jiro died for one page in that book." She matched his volume, not his cold. "I am not letting the tide take it too."

For one breath he stood close enough that her sleeve caught on his cuff, the same accidental intimacy as the canal walk, and then the law remembered its face and he was magistrate again, distance arranged, voice filed.

"Stay inside my shadow on the plank," he said. "One misstep, and the watch writes up two drownings and calls it efficiency."

"They already heard us at the Plum. The barge crew knows we are coming." She lifted her chin. "Good."

"You turned a forgery into theater at the registry." Something like approval moved through him, quick as a blade sheathed. "Now finish the play. You make the noise. I will make the copy. That is a division of labor I can trust."

"Finally," she said, and meant it, "a companion who understands spectacle as cover."

Lanterns swung on the barge rail when they boarded. Deckhands froze. A magistrate on a gangplank was a story that ended in arrests or in bribes, and for three heartbeats they had not decided which.

Kaoru did not give them time. He stepped into the light with a smile like a drawn blade and pinned the factor from the Plum with nothing but stillness and title.

"You. The man who threatened my witness. Step into the light."

The factor stumbled out of the cabin, foreign silver spilling from his sleeve. While Kaoru held him there with words sharp enough to cut, Toa slipped past the awning, fingers finding a waxed folio bound in Scorpion cord. The night smelled of opium resin under honest rice sacks.

"Magistrate-sama." She came back with the manifest before the factor could swallow whatever he kept in his cheek. "Here. Rice in the ledger, resin in the barrels. Jiro was right all along."

"False rice tally, true resin weight." Kaoru never took his eyes off the factor. "Someone taxed this city on its hunger and smuggled poison on the side."

"The Chrysanthemum buys tides, not trials," the factor said. "You have a page. You do not have a name."

"I have enough to hang your chartered concern and every clerk who ever signed for it." Kaoru's voice did not rise. It did not need to. "Run to your flower. The tide will not hide you twice."

He bolted into the warehouse alleys. Deckhands scattered. Only the manifest stayed behind, ink still wet with the lie that had killed a Crane clerk.

---

Morning after the tide, the Scorpion comb and Suzu's testimony and the forged writ and the seized manifest shared one lacquer tray at last. Case One had a spine the watch could not call drowning.

"The Chrysanthemum is still a cipher," Kaoru said. "The factor's concern is not. I will burn his charter before the week is out."

"Jiro gets his name on the watch ledger now," Toa said, "and a line that says strangled. That is a funeral worth standing in the rain for."

"And we still have the traced petals from the Plum." She touched the rice-paper tracing where sixteen petals had betrayed a forger. "The flower has more than one trap waiting for him now."

He looked at her across the desk with an expression she was still learning to read, the one he wore when he had decided something and was not ready to file it where she could see.

"Academy letters sat on the outer desk all night," he said. "Case Two arrived with the dawn, and it is not a polite one."

"Case One, closed." She bowed, witness and artist and woman who had not drowned. "Then whatever the city decides to throw at us next."

Outside, rain returned to the eaves. Somewhere downstream the tide fell, carrying its lies back out to sea.

## VI · Kitchen Seal

*台所の印 (between cases)*

Case One had its spine on the tray now, though this part never earned a number. Call it the kitchen seal: the first night she slept on the far side of his screen, and the midday she tried to cook her gratitude straight into a pot.

---

It was the first night after she moved into the adjoining chamber. The terms were the same ones from the companion appointment: past the inner screen, down the left corridor, and do not, under any circumstances, rearrange his shelves.

Paper kept rustling on his side of the shoji. She lay there counting ceiling beams instead of sheep, and somewhere past the magistrate wing a night clerk stamped a form she would never get to read.

"If you are awake over there," she said into the dark, "you are doing a terrible job of hiding it. The whole city can hear you pacing."

"I can hear you counting from here. Stop it."

"I brought you tea. I know you did not ask for any."

"I did not ask for a chambermate either, and yet here you are. Drink it before it goes cold."

"You still have not said thank you, you know."

"Thank you would imply I wanted the company. What I wanted was a witness who could read a ledger without fainting at the first column."

The footsteps stopped. Fabric whispered. A quilt landed on her side of the screen, heavy wool, unmistakably his, smelling of cedar, ink, and the sleep he would never admit to losing.

"The corridor is still mine. The blanket is only a loan. If you snore, I am reassigning you to the archive."

"Then I will file my breathing under acceptable noise. Good night, Magistrate-sama."

"Good night, To-chan. Case Two starts at dawn, so do try to be vertical by then."

---

Midday after the barge, with Case Two's letters still waiting unopened on the outer desk, the magistrate wing kitchen smelled of curry roux and a little guilt.

"Case One is closed," she said, lining up his bowl and chopsticks with more care than the task deserved, "and you survived a whole tray of my handwriting. So today I am cooking you thank-you curry, not filing a report about it."

"The kitchen is not a courtroom, To-chan. Neither is the lid of that pot."

"I was only lining up the seal. A proper hanko makes everything official, does it not?"

"Official lentils are still just lentils. Put my chop on paper, not on my dinner."

He ate without praise and without flinching, as though his jurisdiction now extended to broth. She watched him finish the whole bowl before she allowed herself to breathe.

"Edible." He set the chopsticks down precisely. "More than I can say for the Academy letters out on the desk. Case Two has been waiting there since this morning."

"Then they can keep waiting until I have washed the pot. You did finish the whole bowl, I noticed."

"I ate the proof that you can follow instructions when you are not stamping the dinner. Do not expect a second serving out of it."

No new case number landed on her desk. Only the steam, and the seal tucked back into his sleeve where it belonged.

"Kitchen seal noted, then. Case Two is next, and I am still right here at your door."

"And you are still in my corridor. That is filing enough for one afternoon."

"Filed. And I promise I will not stamp the ladle again."

## VII · Ashes of Good Tidings

*便りの灰 (Case Two: the Academy packet)*

Case One's tray had not even cooled before the outer bell rang again. Not the watch. Not the Miya envoy. A Crane runner this time, clutching an empty satchel and a story that would not add up no matter how you stacked it.

"Case Two." Kaoru did not look up from the comb. "The Academy was expecting a credential packet for one Kakita Toa. Charter renewal, examination waiver, all the paperwork you people dress up as mercy."

"That satchel is my whole future." Her voice came out thinner than she wanted. "If it is ash, then I crawl back to the Academy in disgrace."

"They found the courier in a warehouse alley. Throat opened, satchel burned to nothing. The watch is calling it robbery. The ash is telling me it was a message."

"You are not running off to beg Crane clerks by letter. You will find what burned, or you will pack for that road I warned you about."

"Then we start where the fire did." She found her spine again. "This time the paper that burned is mine to mourn."

They mapped the courier's route first, bell times and names, because runners did not simply vanish; someone had watched Tsubaki leave the compound, or watched him never arrive. Methodical, Kaoru called it, as if the Academy had taught her something useful after all.

The warehouse district traded honesty for inventory and never felt the loss. Rain had rinsed the cobbles clean, but not the soot. A narrow alley behind a rice broker's kiln still reeked of burned linen and Crane wax.

"The air is wrong here," Toa said. "Thin, like the charms have been peeled away, like someone scraped the protection off these bricks and walked off laughing."

"Or walked off paid. Charms do not come cheap this far from a shrine."

She knelt in the soot and turned up a corner of lacquered board the fire never reached. The Academy's crane-in-flight crest, half melted, still unmistakable.

"Crane wax. They burned the packet and missed the corner of the case." She looked up at him. "Either someone panicked, or someone wanted me to find proof it was ever real."

Rope burns ringed the wrists when they read the body properly. A thin cut tucked under the jaw. Not the showy strangling Jiro wore, but the same hand could have practiced on both.

"His name was Tsubaki. The watch knew it and left it off the report."

Behind the rice sacks a kiln boy finally talked, coin and fear loosening his tongue. A man in Scorpion grey had paid the foreman to keep the kiln hot past curfew. The courier came tearing in, screaming about a seal. Then nothing.

"Scorpion grey, again," Kaoru said. "It seems Case One's little flower is not finished with us yet."

---

The outer desk kept what the alley could not burn. A duplicate routing slip, stamped but never filed, listing the courier's satchel and a second name scribbled in the margin.

"That margin is a clerk's hand," Kaoru said. "Office ink, not warehouse soot. Someone inside my own compound touched the routing before Tsubaki ever set out."

"Case One forged your seal from the outside." She put the slip in his hand and waited, because waiting had become its own kind of courage with him. "This one reaches all the way inside your office."

He read the clerk alone. She stood at the threshold and learned exactly what betrayal in an office sounded like: a stammer, a factor's name, ash and Scorpion wax braided into one confession. Quiet fright, he told her afterward, frightened him more than shouting ever would.

By afternoon the clerk had given up a warehouse foreman, the foreman had given up the Plum factor's cousin, and the cousin had slipped out with the tide. Not before Kaoru inked a duplicate Crane charter on emergency authority, though.

"The Academy gets proof you are still alive, still employed, and still a nuisance." He slid the fresh wax toward her. "That buys you the season."

"Tsubaki deserved better than ash." She kept her voice steady. "I will keep his name even if the watch will not."

"Your decoy snared a rat if we needed one," he added, when the clerk's thread led back to the Chrysanthemum ledger again. "Case One's flower is not done blooming."

"Tonight, you sleep. Tomorrow Case Three arrives whether we invite it or not."

"A charter that survived the fire, and a night to actually sleep." She almost smiled. "I will take it."

"Case Two, closed." She said it like a vow. "The city still lies, but my name gets to stay in Ryoko Owari a little longer."

## VIII · Hill, Fireworks, and Morning Tea

*祭の灯 (Case Two: romance)*

The duplicate charter was barely cool when Kaoru locked the inner screen and slid a festival sash across the desk toward her. Not court dress. Not witness black. Borrowed color, just for the night.

"The outer clerk can guard ash until dawn. You are coming to the hill festival with me. Call it a field inspection of morale."

"Magistrate-sama, Case Two only just closed. The watch will talk if I go parading about in a gold obi."

"The watch files whatever I sign. Tonight I am signing nothing but your attendance. Bring an appetite. The curry stall by the lower bridge owes me a debt."

"You say inspection." She took the sash with fingers that would not quite stay still. "You mean you want me somewhere with lanterns instead of ledgers, where you can keep an eye on me."

"Correct. Now keep up, To-chan."

They slipped out by the service stair. No hanko, no escort line. They climbed the lantern road until the canal city spilled out below them like a tray of scattered coins.

A wooden bench on the overlook faced the whole of Ryoko Owari: paper rivers winding between warehouses, drumbeats thin as a pulse, smoke from food stalls threading up through the eaves.

"Sit." He did not sit until she did. "The hill is mine, by tax map and by habit both. No clerk will come petitioning you up here."

"The whole city is lit up." She had not known it could look kind.

"Kind is what merchants sell when the ledger stays honest for a single night. Enjoy the lie. It is cheaper than virtue."

"Then lie with me until the third bell. I have earned one night that does not stink of ash."

"One night." His mouth did something that was almost gentle. "I know how carefully you count."

Fireworks climbed over the canal. Chrysanthemum bursts, then falling embers that drowned in water wrong-green and lovely. She flinched at the first crack. His hand found her wrist, steady, proprietary.

"Magistrate-sama, everyone down there is watching the sky."

"Let them have the sky. I am watching you remember you are still alive after all that ash."

The next volley painted her white lashes gold. He did not let go.

He kissed her there on the bench. No performance, no ink, only his mouth warm against hers while the fireworks stutter overhead and the city pretended it was innocent.

"Magistrate-sama, not here." She heard herself breathless. "Not with the whole city staring up at us."

"Then choose a door I already hold. Guest room on the way down. One screen, one lock, my corridor."

"One lock. And your hanko stays tucked in your sleeve."

"A dangerous request, from my companion. I will allow it until the wax cools."

What followed stayed off every ledger. Implied, never filed: the breath of shoji grain, lantern light on clothed silhouettes first, then the whisper of fabric, satisfaction with no line to its name. By the time they dressed, the fireworks had thinned to ash on the water. Her obi sat straight again. His cuff was still warm.

"Do not thank me with poetry. Feed yourself before Case Three drags you back to witness notes."

"I already ate. Do not fuss over me." She smiled where he could see it, because hiding hurt worse tonight. "Thank you for the hill, though. Even if the city pretends none of it happened."

"The city pretends every single night. I remember what I pay for."

They ate curry at the stall by the lower bridge anyway, because hunger did not care about dignity, and because he had said the stall owed him a debt and debts in Ryoko Owari were sometimes the only honest currency left.

Lanterns guttered along the descent. Somewhere below, a clerk was already stacking the first paper of Case Three.

"Sleep while you can," Kaoru said. "The next file will not smell of fireworks."

"Then I will dream of ash that did not win for once. Case Three can wait until morning tea."

"It will not wait. Neither will I. When it knocks, you answer it beside me."

---

Dawn through cedar shutters. Duplicate charter cooling on the desk; tea steaming; the outer clerk already transferred to a cell.

"The Academy thinks you are still respectable," Kaoru said. "I think you are still mine to complicate."

"Respectable until noon, then. The rest of me is already yours."

Tea cooled untouched on the desk. They stayed wrapped in the same cushion warmth instead, his thumb on her knuckle, her forehead against his shoulder, neither willing to break the spell first.

"Tsubaki deserved a better city. I deserved not to lose my future in ash."

"You kept both. That is rarer than virtue here."

"Do not make me used to mornings that do not hurt."

"Too late. Case Three will hurt worse. I will be beside you when it does, if you keep choosing me over the door."

"The door can wait. The tea cannot."

## IX · Ledger of the Bolt Room

*絹屋の帳 (Case Three: the fabric shop)*

Case Two's ash was barely a day cold, still clinging to the duplicate charter, when the outer bell rang again. Cloth this time, not blood. A bolt merchant's runner, and the seal in his hand smelled of Scorpion wax.

"Case Three." Kaoru read the chit without pleasure. "That Chrysanthemum factor from Case One did not drown with his barge after all. His cousin sells kimono behind a borrowed Crane name, and the bolt counts never quite agree with the tax lines."

"Fabric instead of dead men in canals." Toa tried for lightness. "That is almost civilized, Magistrate-sama."

"Civilized is how this city hides its knives. You wanted a witness note that outlives the clerks who file it. Earn it in the bolt room, or lose it on my desk."

"You sleep on the other side of my office wall now. So today you walk the shop at my side. Not ahead of me, not alone."

"I heard every word at the festival. Surely Case Three can wait until the tea cools."

"The tea cooled yesterday. The file did not."

The shop smelled of camphor and indigo. Bolts leaned against the walls like courtiers dozing through a dull recital. A clerk in Crane colors bowed a touch too low, smiled a touch too fast.

"Magistrate's custody." Kaoru did not raise his voice. "Open the bolt room. No one lays a finger on the top shelf until my witness has signed the count."

"The third bell has not even rung." Toa kept her hands visible, open. "So why is the brace on that shelf fresh-cut?"

"Because someone wanted the inventory to come down the moment the right person stood beneath it." He was already moving toward the street door, or so she thought. "Ask your questions. I will be close enough to hear you scream, far enough that the clerks still think you are alone."

"You are leaving me in the aisle."

"I am handing you a clerk who will talk to a dancer and lie to a magistrate. Use that."

He vanished from the aisle the way he always vanished when he wanted the room to misread him. She matched dye tags on the bolt ends against the ledger and found crimson tagged Crane on the outside, Scorpion vat thread on the inside. Someone had switched labels before the tax count.

"This bolt belongs in Case One's file," she said, "not on some merchant's shelf."

The clerk's hands shook when she asked for the wax on the second ledger hidden under the counter. Night counts that would never match the day book Kaoru was about to seize at the door.

"You signed two different truths. Decide now which one drowns when the magistrate reads them both."

Beneath the old lacquer the shelf brace showed a clean saw kerf. Deliberate. Recent. The top shelf leaned a finger's width toward the aisle.

"This whole room is a trap wearing inventory for a costume."

The clerk excused himself to fetch fresh seals. The street door latched. Overhead, rope began to groan.

A lantern swayed. The top bolt shifted, pale silk spilling loose like a white serpent uncoiling, and the shelf timber screamed.

She had one breath and one choice that mattered.

"Magistrate-sama!"

He was already moving. Not at the street door at all, but inside the line of the fall, coat thrown back, one arm hooking hard under her obi. They hit the mat together, sideways, silk dragging across her cheek, his breath hot against her temple, his heartbeat suddenly louder than any of the clerk's lies.

"Up. Now." His voice was ice again. "Before that clerk invents a story where you died clumsy."

"Your hands are shaking."

"A draft, from the alley. Nerves. Stand."

For one whole heartbeat his grip did not loosen. Grey eyes locked on hers, too honest, every ounce of performance stripped away.

"If you had died in my bolt room, I would have burned this entire quarter down to the canal."

"Magistrate-sama..."

"Forget I said that. Witness note: shelf sabotage, clerk detained. You are breathing. That is the only romance this file requires."

"I am not, that is not what, I will write the shelf line first."

"Good. Devotion stays on paper until I say otherwise."

---

By afternoon the clerk had given up the Chrysanthemum cousin's runner, the runner had given up payment for the shelf, and Kaoru inked a custody chit thick enough to survive one jealous factor's lawyers.

"Case Three is not closed. It is pinned. Like the bolt that almost killed my witness."

"The mistagged lot ties straight back to Case One's flower." He nodded at her notes. "Paste that line into the file before some Scorpion lawyer buys the clerk's silence."

"I still hear the shelf coming down every time I close my eyes. Thank you for being at the mat, not the door."

"I was at the mat, not the door. Do not make poetry of it. Case Four will knock soon enough, the moment the rain comes back."

Case Three's first line was written into the file. The city still lied. She was still breathing in Ryoko Owari.

## X · Is This a Date?

*涙島の夜 (Case 3.5: Tear Drop Island)*

Case Three's saw kerf still smelled of cedar dust when Kaoru set down his brush. Not on a docket this time, but on a folded sheet of rice paper, sealed with wax that carried no office chop at all.

"The clerk can run until dawn for all I care. You, on the other hand, have an appointment that is not on any ledger."

"Magistrate-sama, Case Four is going to be steel at the dock. If I vanish in festival dress, the watch will file nothing but gossip about it."

"The watch files what I sign. And tonight I am signing nothing except your attendance."

He slid the paper across the desk. The hand was unmistakably his, blunt vertical strokes, not a breath of poetry in it.

*Meet me on Tear Drop Island. There is an inn on the north side, tucked between two geisha houses. Wear your best.*

"Wear my..." She could not finish.

"Read the rest once you have remembered how to breathe."

The second line was written smaller: *Performances available upon request from either neighboring house. Do not be late.*

"You bought a whole venue. You did not buy me a single reason why."

"You will have your reason when you arrive, and arrive hungry. Keep up, To-chan."

She agonized over every last hairpin, dithered between obi brocades for hours, and finally flipped a coin just to settle on her shoes. Spring's turning let her play with patterns the peak seasons would never allow: black furisode scattered with grape hyacinth at the roots of a cherry tree climbing her sleeves, rose-gold obi brocade tied in a musubi that trailed past her hips, zori instead of clunky geta, kanzashi bells that sang whenever she moved.

Rouge on her lips and at her eyes. Deliberate grace tonight, instead of her usual exuberant freedom. At one-third of her normal stride, the walk to the ferry took forever.

Tear Drop Island at night was all lantern-gold on dark water, licensed-quarter elegance without a trace of the magistrate hall's ink. The geisha house eaves leaned close over the canal like confidantes sharing a secret.

The inn sat exactly where he said it would. Not another guest on the walkway. Not a sound of chatter behind the main screen.

"The whole place?!"

A servant offered crisp hojicha, the kind she would chase down again for weeks afterward. When she asked, they only bowed: yes, Magistrate Kitsu had taken the entire venue. Performances on request, from either neighbor.

"So no one else is here at all."

Inside: tatami, paper lanterns, a low table set for two. Biwa music threaded through from behind the wall, not a performance yet, only atmosphere.

"Good evening, Magistrate-sama!"

She tried to bow the way she always did. The stiff fukuro obi granted her barely a hinge. She laughed to herself and straightened, catching his fragrance, the very same note as that first wrong door.

"Good evening, Toa-san. The journey here was not too difficult, I hope?"

"No, only longer than I expected. I do hope you were not waiting too long." She heard herself add, smaller, "You smell so nice."

"Hm. Are you hungry? Thirsty?"

She tipped her face upward, checking in with her own body. "Both!"

"The menu has already been decided for you."

Kaiseki trays arrived, spring's turning laid out on porcelain: asparagus with salted plum, skipjack with mustard greens, white miso and fiddleheads. She whispered *itadakimasu* and went quiet the moment fiddlehead met her tongue, nothing but a soft hum of pleasure.

"Eeeee, fiddleheads! I am so excited!"

"Enjoy it."

The sake was poured, and he did not throw it back. It was expensive enough to be savored.

"I have arranged for more music, and dancers, should that suit your desires."

"I have not listened to music, or watched dancing, just for the joy of it in such a long time. This is really lovely, Magistrate-sama. Kampai!"

"Kampai."

Between bites she watched him savor each flavor the way he savored a confession, unhurried, proprietary, pleased without ever performing that pleasure for anyone else.

When they had polished off two dishes apiece, more instruments came in beneath the biwa. At the first drumbeats she perked up, slate eyes half-transfixed and half-evaluating, an old authority settling back into her shoulders.

"If I ever turn up a dance crime," he said, "at least I will know exactly who to bring with me."

"Relax. Enjoy the dance. Unless the watching is the fun of it. What could they be doing better?"

"They are beautiful. That is more than enough for one night."

"Witness approved."

Later, sake warm in her veins, she heard herself say things she had been carrying like stones in her sleeve.

"People still ask me, at least once a week, whether I am alright. Whether you are hurting me. Whether I stay of my own free will. I tell them I have never once felt unsafe with you. That you actually weigh what I say. That we see each other clearly, without lying..."

She caught herself on the edge of a word she would not let herself name until Case Five.

"So, um. The point is, bodies do not lie, and I have always trusted..."

She turned to her plate instead. Then the sake. Then the air.

"I know." His voice was gentler than she expected. "But now you are going to tell me who has been saying these things. I insist."

"W-well, Hi-Hiromi-san. And Doji S-Sango-san..."

"Doji Sango-san? Have the two of us even spoken?"

He leaned back, delighted, treating the rumor as intelligence, not shame.

"If it is only two names, then I clearly need to work on my image."

"It is not only two. I simply cannot summon any more while my heart is sprinting like this."

"And what else has this Doji Sango-san been saying?"

"Nothing beyond that one basket of topics. I do not know her well."

"Oh. Now I am very interested."

"I think you may simply have to ask her directly, Magistrate-sama."

"I would never do such a thing. That would be admitting defeat."

"Should I stop telling people that I stay freely? Did I do something wrong?"

"No. Do not change a single thing. If servants are carrying it, she may as well be keeping watchers."

She ate sesame seeds one by one, then asked the question that turned itself over in her chest.

"Do you hear bad things about me?"

"Never once. You are very well loved in this city."

"Well, that is good!"

"Dessert?"

"Of course! What is a fancy meal without it?"

Bean paste with tea. Sweet enough to let them pretend there was no dock steel waiting for them at dawn.

---

They walked the lantern path down toward the ferry, her zori careful on the stone, his pace slowed to match her bells and her obi.

"You bought the whole island. You fed me fiddleheads. You said that I am loved."

"I said the city speaks well of my witness. Do not go inflating the file."

"Magistrate-sama, when you plan out an entire evening like tonight, what do you call it?"

She could not swallow the question forever.

"Is this a date?"

"A date is a line in a permit book."

Pause.

"This is appreciation. For witness competence. And food, so you do not faint on my desk tomorrow."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the answer the quarter deserves. You are a patronage artist, the woman who trusted me in the bolt room. Do not go borrowing festival vocabulary just because the sake was expensive."

"The festival was real. Your hand on that cup was real. Do not tell me to un-feel it just because the file would prefer silence."

"Feel whatever you like in private. In public, you are mine to position."

"...No."

"It is the closest I come, before I learn better. Forget the second sentence. The first one stands."

Crane training kept her face pleasant. Inside, something unnamed ached, not love, not by name, only the quiet wish that he would stop stepping back after every step he took forward.

His hand lifted. Not to take hers, but to straighten a lantern cord near her sleeve. His fingers paused a breath's width from her palm. The almost-touch hurt worse than any refusal.

The compound gate closed behind them. Cedar and ink took the place of hojicha and drum. She should sleep. Instead she lay there listening to his footsteps fade toward the inner hall, still not a date by his ledger, still not love by its name, still hers in every file he refused to romanticize.

"Tea. Then bed. The dock tomorrow will not smell of lanterns."

"Yes, Magistrate-sama."

"And, To-chan. Do not sulk in the witness log. Sulk in private. I permit you that much."

## XI · Blade at the Dock

*剣の帳 (Case Four: Kurogane)*

The fabric-shop clerk's runner never made it to the canal office. A dock watchman swore the man had bolted for the lower piers with Case Three's night ledger under his arm, grey wax on its cord still wet.

"Case Four is where the paper trail runs out and the steel begins," Kaoru said. "We raid the lower dock tonight. Bring me Kurogane, or bring me his blood."

"I am a witness. Not a yoriki."

"Tonight you are both. Stay in my line, or the file learns that you did not."

Rain hammered the compound before dawn. Case Three's custody chit was barely dry when he pinned a dock watchman's note beside the bolt-room saw kerf. Lower pier. Warehouse three. A foreman who signed his name in Scorpion grey.

"Kurogane runs the split crates our clerk ran toward. He learned his trade under a Scorpion quartermaster, so he will not surrender to a brush and ink."

"And me? Another bolt room to comb through, or steel this time?"

"Steel. You dance with a blade when I tell you to dance. You do not duel a foreman on your own."

"You learned how to breathe in my bolt room." His mouth quirked. "Tonight you breathe in my line, or the ledger learns that you could not."

"I am not your decoration."

"Correct. You are a witness with a wakizashi. Walk like you know the difference."

The lower piers smelled of tar rope and split crates and that wrong shade of green breathing off the canal. Lanterns guttered in the rain. The mouth of the warehouse swallowed every sound.

"Three breaths." Kaoru drew his katana without drama. "Read the room. Then we take him."

Grey wax on a split crate. The sixteenth-petal forgery again. Case One's flower had not drowned; it had moved into a warehouse.

Foreman Kurogane. Scorpion pay, Crane crates for cover. He bolted the moment the clerk did, and when he stepped out of the crate shadow his blade was already drawn. He did not bow.

"The magistrate's pet and his little dancer. So the clerk talked. I am not walking to the pleasure quarter in chains."

"Then talk to my witness note. After you sheathe that blade."

"Witnesses die quiet in Ryoko Owari."

Three blades in the rain. Kaoru's katana carved a clean arc. Toa's wakizashi rode her dance-line. Kurogane's Scorpion cut kept trying to split them apart. Crate wood splintered. The footing underneath was blood-slick tar.

"Toa! Hold the line!"

Kurogane feinted high, then dropped low, steel angled for her ribs instead of his. One breath to choose.

"Stay behind me, Magistrate-sama."

She dropped back a single step. Not cowardice. Kaoru crossed her line without even looking, and his coat took the foreman's edge exactly where her obi would have torn.

Steel screamed once. Kurogane stumbled into the custody rope, not into mercy. Kaoru's sleeve was dark with rain, or with something worse, and still his free hand found her wrist and pinned her behind his shoulder.

"Breathe. Count to three. You are still mine to file."

"You took the cut that was meant for me."

"Draft in the alley. Nerves. Stand."

For one heartbeat his grip did not loosen. Grey eyes on hers, too honest, stripped of performance.

"If you had died on my dock, I would have burned this whole pier down to the canal."

"Magistrate-sama..."

"Forget I said it. Witness note: foreman detained, ledger seized. You are still breathing, and that is the only romance the file requires tonight."

"I am not, that is not... I will write up the blade line first."

"Good. Devotion belongs on paper until I say otherwise."

---

By dawn the foreman's manifest sat in custody beside Case Three's saw kerf. The clerk's runner had not vanished, only gone quiet, and in Ryoko Owari that quiet was its own kind of confession.

"Case Four is pinned, not closed. The flower still has its roots."

"You stayed in my line when the steel wanted you alone. Do not expect applause. Expect a pleasure-quarter seal stolen by morning."

"I stayed because the file said witness. Not because..."

"Because is a word for Case Five. Tonight you sleep."

"Case Four's line is written. The city still lies, but I am still at your door."

## XII · Small Boats

*舟の帳 (Case 4.5: the kobune)*

Case Four's ink was barely dry. Winter Scorpion raids had the harbor clerks lying about grain again, and Tear Drop Island's marina still owed a witness line from the dock spill.

"I can take the island manifest before dawn," Toa said. "The ferry masters always file late when they are frightened."

"Good. Wear a cloak. Leave the ledger. If the watch asks, you are inventory I chose not to file yet."

"That is not how permits work, and you know it."

"Permits are just paper. Boats are honest. Keep up, To-chan."

Tear Drop Island's marina was nothing like the licensed quarter's polished stone. Just winter ribs and broken pilings. The bay wind cut straight through the rubble. A small kobune rocked at the tide line, its paint scarred from somebody else's war.

She peered out from behind a split mooring post, witness notes tucked into her sleeve. Then a familiar voice cracked across the cold.

"Can just ONE thing go right tonight?!"

The Emerald Magistrate, soaked and furious, in a boat, in this freezing cold? Bay water and not rain. He ran the kobune aground and kicked the hull once, hard, like the wood had personally betrayed him.

"Magistrate-sama!"

She scrambled over the wreckage, hair loose, dry kosode whipping in shore wind.

"Gods, are you alright? What in the world happened to you?"

Her grey eyes searched his face. Her warm fingertips brushed his chilled cheeks before she could think better of it.

He held up one hand. A glare, not a dismissal. He kept wringing out his robes, plain working attire, nothing court-fine. He did not tell her to leave.

She stepped back, arms falling to her sides. A sniffle. Then a whimper. Tears spilled down her wind-reddened cheeks, concern he would not let her offer, and she still did not know if he was whole.

"Bad day for you too, then?"

He stripped out of soaked layers right there on the cold shore, not for show, only to survive. The scene spared what the bay already stole.

"A bad day? Yes. Yes, it has been a bad day! Calamity and hunger and suffering, and that spooky nonsense in the wards, and now you, you almost sank a boat, and it is so cold, and you could have died..."

She crumpled, and a wordless wail slipped out between hitched breaths.

"I am fine, To-chan. Just cold."

"...why are you even out here in a boat?"

"There is no other way to reach Tear Drop Island. Obviously."

He wrapped his arms around himself, furious, exposed, well past politeness now.

"Are you going to stand there gawking, or are you going to come warm me up? Come here. It has been a bad day, and you, To-chan, get to deal with it."

She wiped her face once and folded him into a hug. Her wide sleeves draped over him like a blanket. She was just slightly taller, so the warmth finally crossed the gap between witness and man.

"Almost enough." He dragged her toward the kobune. "But not quite."

"The deck of a boat is not a tatami mat."

"Worse."

He sent her over the side, into the hull, not into the canal. The kobune rocked hard. Water slapped at the strakes.

She went limp and let the hull take her. Knees, hips, shoulder, all wet wood. Pain and surprise in a single breath. The roll underneath was wrong, almost dizzying.

"You are on top today. I am too cold to manage it, and you have feelings to work through besides. So get to it."

"O-oh. Alright then."

What followed stayed off every ledger. Implied, never filed: the hull rocking, his palms bracing the gunwales whenever the boat threatened to tip, hunger without a ledger line, breath against her hair when words would have been too honest for the marina air.

When the rhythm finally ended, they were a single knot of warmth and borrowed robes. The bay cold waited just outside the strakes.

"...You did not, though. You did not find me."

She lifted her head, grey eyes locking onto his.

"I found you. Do you remember?"

"Hm? Oh. I suppose that is true. Were you really looking for someone like me, though?"

"I went looking for a magistrate to handle my paperwork." She looked away, and for once he did not scold her. "But I found Kitsu Kaoru, the man."

His full name left her mouth before she could swallow it back.

"Mostly I wonder why more women cannot be like you. So willing to let a man like me bully them along."

"Your color is coming back. Can we please go home now?"

"Fiiiiiiiine."

"A bath, and mikan under the kotatsu. That is the treaty."

"Toa-san? I am going to bend you over the side of that bath."

They ferried back before the watch could invent a drowning. Steam and citrus filled the magistrate wing, the bath, the kotatsu, mikan peeled in a silence that was almost domestic.

"Case Five will not wait just because you bruised your knees on my kobune."

"Yes, Magistrate-sama. The witness line is written."

"And To-chan, do your sulking in private if you must. The marina saw quite enough of us tonight."

## XIII · The Left Hand

*御前の帳 (Case Five: yoriki)*

Three mornings after the dock finally went quiet, the runner skipped breakfast. He brought a summons instead, ink still wet, pleasure-quarter seal copied twice like the clerk was afraid someone would call it a forgery.

"Magistrate-sama, the runner would not meet my eyes. Does that mean the witness line is finished?"

"Witness is a word for paper that has not earned ink yet. Case Five opens at the hour of the serpent. Dress for court, not for my corridor."

"Court sounds like dismissal. Or punishment. You said when the files closed we would talk about terms."

"We are talking about them. In a room that can hear you."

She packed Crane neatness into a travel kosode, the kind you wore when patronage ran out and the gate guard asked for papers you no longer had. Her hands shook on the fold.

"Leave the adjoining chamber key at the gate. Today you are witness. The hall does not care what you called last night."

"Then I will attend as your witness one last time. The hall taught me how to stand where I am told."

"Good. That pleasure-quarter seal on the summons is bait for clerks who think Case Five is only Chrysanthemum politics. It is not. Stay in my line."

---

In the office before the hall, he did not pretend it had been charity.

"Case One, you read a lie under pressure at the Lacquered Plum and you did not run when the comb turned out to be Scorpion."

"Case Two, you stood on the hill where the city could see you beside my file, and you did not hide behind Crane honor when kiln ash choked the alley."

"Case Three, you trusted the bolt room when the room tried to kill you. You let me pull you out instead of proving the Academy right on splinters."

"Case Four, you stayed behind my shoulder when steel wanted you alone. You did not compose poetry on my dock."

"You never told me any of that was a test."

"I said provisional attachment until the files close. The files did not close themselves, To-chan."

"I thought you meant stipend. A permit. A room next to yours."

"I meant a left hand. The city needs a name on it. I chose yours before you knew what the job was called."

---

Ryoko Owari's open docket hall smelled of wet stone and copied petitions. Quarter clerks lined the left wall. A Crane envoy's crest sat cold on the right. Someone in the back row wore another magistrate's colors but never took the seat.

"Kitsu Kaoru, Emerald Magistrate." His voice carried without shouting. "Case Five: witness chains for the Chrysanthemum root, and appointment of sworn yoriki for the ongoing docket."

"Yoriki is police rank." Her mouth was dry. "That is not the same as a dancer's permit."

"Witness is not enough in this city. You signed my corridor, my cushion, my release. The quarter whispers kept woman. The file will say office."

The Crane envoy did not stand. His silence read like a letter home to the Crane courts.

"Kakita Toa. Appointment as yoriki to the Emerald office of Ryoko Owari. Witness chains, quarter escort, left-hand signature on my docket."

"Magistrate-sama, Crane custom expects an honor guard and clan auditors at my side. Not a magistrate's toy left on a public desk."

"You already served the desk. You slept behind my screen. You argued well when I wanted competence within reach. This is office. I am not asking for poetry. I need a name the city can cite when the seal walks."

She looked at the scroll, at the wax, at the man who had dated her future forward and cooked nothing into curry and thrown her into a kobune and called it appreciation until the word almost broke her.

"I accept the appointment and its duties." Her seal met the line. "You will have my discretion and my attendance."

"You passed every case I put in front of you. Do not make me say it twice in front of clerks who sell courage by the cup."

"I will not embarrass your file."

The envoy exhaled once, not a blessing, just math. The rival magistrate's colors left before the wax cooled.

---

The inner office after public ink felt smaller than the hearing hall. He locked the corridor himself. No runner, no cushion joke.

"You may speak without the docket listening. One sentence you never filed, and one you still owe me."

"I needed the permit to stay close, but I chose to stay on purpose. I could have run. Then you could have caught me."

"I know."

"You are not easy."

"Most people try to rename me. You lay on your back and still came back to the desk."

"In the bedroom I can be your toy. Out there, I want to stand beside you."

"Then stand. The oath is public. The rest is mine to file when you stop stammering."

What followed stayed off every ledger. Implied, never filed: behind his screen, where Crane purity was not a performance and wanting him honestly did not require him to be kinder, only there.

"Magistrate-sama, still yours."

"Still mine. That is the only title that matters tonight."

---

The first week as his sworn left hand was not a montage of victory. It was runners, copied seals, and a quarter walk where the okami nodded at her sash instead of her obi.

"File the witness chain before noon. The Chrysanthemum root still has tendrils in the pleasure quarter."

"Yes, Magistrate-sama. The line is written, and I am still at your door."

"Good. Case Five is pinned, not closed. The city still lies."

She did not say love. He did not offer it. Ryoko Owari had a name on his left hand, and a week that had only begun.

## Epilogue · Off the Ledger

*炬燵の午後 (bonus)*

Afternoon off the ledger: no case number, no runner, only steam under the kotatsu and mikan peel curling on the tray.

"Yoriki paperwork says I must initial every quarter escort in triplicate. Your clerks invented a fourth copy while I bathed."

"Clerks sell courage by the cup. You sell me indignation by the segment."

"I am teasing. You may almost smile if you try."

"Almost is the only concession this office allows before sunset."

The corner of his mouth twitched. Not quite a smile.

"Good. I will file that under victories too small for the docket."

"Eat your mikan. If you drip on the yoriki sash, I will make you copy the stain description twice."

She passed him a segment anyway. He took it without looking, as if hunger were also classified information.

"No new case tonight. No seal stolen before dawn. Just this room."

"Just this room is still mine to share when the city is not listening."

"Then I will stay where the ledger cannot count."

Rain ticked the eaves. The kotatsu held their warmth and the peel and the almost-smile he would deny tomorrow. Ryoko Owari lied elsewhere. Here, for once, it did not matter.
